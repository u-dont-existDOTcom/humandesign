# Theory-Neutral Behavioral Measurement Codebook

## 1. Purpose and scope

This codebook describes recurring behavioral patterns that can be supported by first-person accounts of concrete situations. It codes what a person reports perceiving, experiencing, choosing, doing, not doing, repeating, or changing. It does not assign personality types, diagnoses, moral evaluations, demographic explanations, biological explanations, or unobserved motives.

The observable groups below are organizational aids only. They are not proposed dimensions, latent traits, scales, or components of a personality model. No composite score should be created from them without a separate empirical measurement-development process.

## 2. Unit of coding

The primary unit is a **behavioral episode**. An episode should identify as much of the following sequence as the narrative permits:

1. The situation or opportunity.
2. What the person knew, noticed, or experienced at the time.
3. What actions were available or perceived as available.
4. What the person did or did not do.
5. The order and timing of actions.
6. What happened next.
7. Conditions that affected the behavior.
8. Relevant counterexamples or exceptions.

An episode need not contain every element to support every observable. The **Required evidence** field for each observable determines the minimum information needed.

### Opportunity requirement

Non-action is behaviorally informative only when the narrative supports all three conditions:

- the person was aware of the relevant opportunity, request, problem, or choice;
- action was reasonably feasible within a defined period; and
- non-action is explicitly reported or directly entailed by the chronology.

When feasibility or awareness is unknown, use **insufficient evidence**, not an avoidance, delay, refusal, or noncompliance value.

## 3. Evidence forms

Coders should distinguish four evidence forms:

- **Concrete episode:** A bounded situation with reported behavior and enough sequence information to code the observable.
- **Anchored repeated-series report:** A claim such as “I did this every week” accompanied by at least one concrete example and enough information to identify the recurring opportunity and behavior.
- **Global self-description:** A statement such as “I am decisive” or “I never ask for help.” Record it as a claim, but do not treat it as confirmation without behavioral episodes.
- **Narrator explanation:** A stated reason such as “I left because I felt ignored.” The reported explanation may be retained, but coders must not extend it into an unreported hidden cause.

Directly reported perceptions and internal experiences may be coded when an observable explicitly requires them. They do not, by themselves, establish what action followed.

## 4. Universal coding states

Every observable record must permit the following state values. These are separate from the observable’s substantive values.

- **observed:** The required evidence supports the specified substantive value at the stated scope. A single complete episode can be observed at the episode level.
- **contradicted:** A clearly specified candidate pattern or self-description is directly opposed by qualifying behavioral evidence at the same scope. The incompatible observed value and episode must be recorded. This state does not mean that the person “lacks” a quality.
- **mixed:** Two or more meaningfully different substantive values have qualifying evidence. Context-linked differences must be retained rather than averaged.
- **insufficient evidence:** Required elements are missing, feasibility is uncertain, the sequence is ambiguous, or there are too few episodes to support a recurring-pattern claim.
- **not applicable:** The available material contains no genuine opportunity for the observable to occur. This is not the same as the behavior being unmentioned.

A universal statement can be contradicted by one clear counterexample. A context-bound statement is contradicted only by evidence from a sufficiently comparable context. A frequency statement cannot be evaluated without an appropriate opportunity denominator.

## 5. Recurrence and aggregation rules

A complete episode may support an episode-level value, but it does not establish a recurring person-level pattern.

A provisional recurring pattern requires either:

- at least two distinct qualifying episodes with the same substantive value; or
- one detailed episode plus an anchored repeated-series report establishing at least one additional occurrence.

Person-level summaries must report:

- the substantive values observed;
- the number and type of qualifying opportunities when available;
- the contexts in which each value appeared;
- every material counterexample;
- whether the result is observed, contradicted, mixed, insufficient, or not applicable.

Do not average incompatible values into a midpoint. A suitable summary is:

> `OBS011: starts at first feasible opportunity in two urgent family episodes; repeated unplanned delay in three self-scheduled administrative episodes; state = mixed; documented modifier = external urgency and domain.`

It would be inappropriate to convert that record into “medium initiation.”

## 6. Standard coding record

A recommended record contains:

`Person ID | Episode ID | Observable ID | Opportunity present/absent/unclear | Substantive value(s), in sequence | Coding state | Evidence span | Timing | Outcome | Context modifiers | Counterepisode links | Coder note`

Multiple values may be recorded in temporal order, such as:

`seek information → limited trial → full entry`

All observables permit **other specified**, accompanied by a concrete behavioral description. It should not be used in place of **insufficient evidence**.

## 7. Common context modifiers

Record modifiers only when supported by the narrative. Common modifiers include:

- domain or activity;
- voluntariness versus obligation;
- familiarity or novelty;
- relationship closeness;
- role or power asymmetry;
- public versus private setting;
- time pressure;
- social scrutiny;
- stakes and reversibility;
- availability of information or assistance;
- financial, material, or time constraints;
- fatigue, illness, pain, or competing workload;
- reported strong emotion;
- prior outcomes in similar situations;
- cultural, organizational, family, or community norms as described by the narrator;
- life period or historical timing.

Context modifiers describe conditions. They should not be treated as causes unless the episode directly supports the connection.

# CODEBOOK

## Entering and reading situations

### OBS001 — Optional engagement response

1. **Name:** Optional engagement response.
2. **Operational definition:** The person’s first observable response when aware of a genuine option to enter, accept, explore, or participate in a situation, activity, opportunity, or interaction.
3. **Include when:** The narrative establishes awareness of the opportunity, meaningful optionality, and what the person did next.
4. **Do not include when:** Participation was compulsory; the person did not know about the opportunity; the episode concerns starting an already accepted task; or only a global claim such as “I am adventurous” is provided.
5. **Possible values:** Enters immediately; seeks information then enters; enters on a limited or trial basis; defers until a stated condition; enters after prompting or obligation increases; explicitly declines; avoids or does not respond despite a feasible opportunity; delegates the entry decision to another person.
6. **Required evidence:** A defined opportunity, evidence that the person knew it was available, some indication of optionality, and an observed response within a meaningful period.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable episodes in which the person responds differently. A pattern of immediate entry is qualified by episodes of deliberate trial entry or explicit decline, even when outcomes differ.
8. **Context modifiers:** Familiarity, cost, reversibility, social invitation, expected time demand, stakes, prior experience, available companions, and whether declining required an explicit response.
9. **Examples:**
   - A workshop place becomes available. Mara checks the schedule and registers that afternoon: **seeks information then enters**.
   - Devin says, “I usually avoid new groups,” but gives no episode involving an available group: recurrence is **insufficient evidence**.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring fear, confidence, curiosity, or sociability; treating a successful outcome as evidence of willingness to engage; or coding silence as avoidance without establishing awareness and feasibility.

### OBS002 — Reported cue guiding the next action

1. **Name:** Reported cue guiding the next action.
2. **Operational definition:** The specific event, perception, communication, remembered experience, rule, or internal state that the person reports noticing and using in selecting the next action.
3. **Include when:** A cue and a subsequent action are both reported, and their connection is explicit or directly established by the sequence.
4. **Do not include when:** The detail was mentioned only as background; the coder believes the person “must have noticed” it; or the narrator supplies a broad retrospective interpretation without connecting it to a particular action.
5. **Possible values:** Environmental change; another person’s words or behavior; rule, deadline, or prior commitment; anticipated consequence; reported bodily or emotional state; remembered prior episode; routine time, place, or sequence cue; multiple cues used together.
6. **Required evidence:** Identification of what was noticed or recalled, the action that followed, and a direct linguistic or chronological link between them.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes in which the same cue is present but does not guide action, or in which the person explicitly acts against it. Different cue use across domains qualifies any general pattern.
8. **Context modifiers:** Cue visibility, reliability, novelty, competing cues, time lag, familiarity, role expectations, and whether the cue was public or private.
9. **Examples:**
   - When Imani sees that the road is flooded, she turns around and calls the venue: **environmental change guiding action**.
   - A manager’s tone sounds important to the coder, but the speaker never mentions noticing it: do not code that tone as an action-guiding cue.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Coding every detail in an episode as attended to; inferring motives from the cue; or treating an unmentioned cue as one the person failed to notice.

---

### OBS003 — Information seeking before action

1. **Name:** Information seeking before action.
2. **Operational definition:** Deliberate behavior used to acquire information before making or implementing a consequential choice.
3. **Include when:** The person identifies an unanswered question and performs, delegates, or deliberately forgoes an information-acquisition step before acting.
4. **Do not include when:** Information is sought only after the decision; the activity is casual exploration with no identified decision; the person seeks practical assistance rather than information; or the coder infers an information need that the person did not report.
5. **Possible values:** Direct observation or inspection; asks a knowledgeable person; consults a document or source; compares multiple sources; conducts a small information-gathering test; delegates information gathering; explicitly relies on existing information and proceeds; explicitly forgoes available information.
6. **Required evidence:** A decision-relevant unknown, a concrete search or deliberate non-search action, and its temporal position before the decision or larger action.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable decisions made without information seeking, reliance on only one previously rejected source, or episodes in which information is gathered but not used. These qualify recurrence but do not establish why the behavior differed.
8. **Context modifiers:** Stakes, source access, expertise, time pressure, source trust as reported, familiarity, reversibility, and cost of searching.
9. **Examples:**
   - Before approving a repair, Lin calls two repair shops and compares their explanations: **compares multiple sources**.
   - After accepting the repair, Lin reads several articles but does not reconsider the decision: not pre-action information seeking for that choice.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Judging source quality instead of coding behavior; treating reassurance as information without evidence of decision relevance; or coding “I thought about it” as information acquisition.

---

### OBS004 — Action under unresolved uncertainty

1. **Name:** Action under unresolved uncertainty.
2. **Operational definition:** What the person does when a recognized, decision-relevant unknown remains unresolved at the point action is possible.
3. **Include when:** The person is aware of a meaningful unknown, cannot or does not resolve it fully, and then takes or withholds a concrete action.
4. **Do not include when:** The uncertainty was resolved before action; uncertainty is inferred only by the coder; or the episode reports information gathering but ends before the person faces an action point.
5. **Possible values:** Proceeds with the full action; conducts a limited or reversible trial; delays pending information or a condition; follows an established default; transfers or shares the decision; maintains multiple options; declines or exits; takes no action during the defined opportunity.
6. **Required evidence:** The unresolved unknown, the person’s awareness of it, an identifiable action opportunity, and the action or explicit non-action that followed.
7. **Counterevidence:** Similar unresolved situations producing different responses. A limited trial in one high-stakes setting and full action in another low-stakes setting should be retained as a context split.
8. **Context modifiers:** Consequence severity, reversibility, available backup, deadline, responsibility for others, familiarity, prior uncertainty outcomes, and authority structure.
9. **Examples:**
   - Unsure whether new software will alter old files, Kojo copies one file and tests it before migrating the rest: **limited or reversible trial**.
   - A schedule later proves uncertain, but the interview never establishes that Rina knew this beforehand: insufficient evidence for this observable.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Equating uncertainty with indecision; assuming that action means certainty; or coding delay without establishing that uncertainty was still unresolved.

---

### OBS005 — Exposure to a recognized possible loss

1. **Name:** Exposure to a recognized possible loss.
2. **Operational definition:** How the person sets the scale, reversibility, or distribution of an action after recognizing a possible negative consequence.
3. **Include when:** The narrative establishes that a downside was known before action and that the person chose how much exposure to accept.
4. **Do not include when:** Risk is apparent only to the coder; the possible loss was discovered after the action; no alternative level of exposure was available; or the account merely labels the action “risky.”
5. **Possible values:** Declines the exposure; caps the amount or duration; stages exposure incrementally; creates a backup or hedge; shares or transfers exposure; accepts the full identified exposure; increases exposure after favorable evidence; leaves the amount of exposure to another person.
6. **Required evidence:** A specifically recognized downside, timing showing awareness before action, at least one feasible alternative, and the actual exposure-setting behavior.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable episodes with a different degree or method of exposure. A single large exposure does not contradict systematic limiting in unrelated low-cost situations unless the opportunities are comparable.
8. **Context modifiers:** Magnitude and type of possible loss, reversibility, available resources, responsibility for others, prior losses, social obligations, time horizon, and availability of insurance or backup.
9. **Examples:**
   - Salma knows a project may fail, commits only one month of funds, and retains an emergency reserve: **caps exposure and creates a backup**.
   - A mountain route is objectively hazardous, but the speaker reports no awareness of the hazard before choosing it: do not infer a risk-exposure pattern.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Converting the code into “risk tolerance”; using the outcome to decide whether the original choice was risky; or treating financially large and personally consequential exposures as automatically comparable.

## Choosing and starting

### OBS006 — Option generation

1. **Name:** Option generation.
2. **Operational definition:** The observable process by which possible courses of action become part of the person’s consideration set before a choice.
3. **Include when:** The narrative identifies one or more alternatives and provides information about how those alternatives were produced or obtained.
4. **Do not include when:** Alternatives are reconstructed by the coder; only the final choice is known; or multiple actions occurred sequentially without having been considered as alternatives.
5. **Possible values:** Considers one stated option; generates several options independently; adds options sequentially as the episode unfolds; solicits options from others; starts from an existing menu or constrained set; accepts a default without searching for alternatives; combines elements of several options.
6. **Required evidence:** A choice point, identifiable alternatives or an explicit absence of alternative search, and enough sequence information to determine how the option set arose.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes showing a wider, narrower, or differently sourced option set. A self-description such as “I always consider every possibility” is contradicted by concrete decisions made from an unexamined default.
8. **Context modifiers:** Time available, expertise, option visibility, social input, stakes, novelty, institutional rules, and whether options could be added later.
9. **Examples:**
   - Faced with a cancelled trip, Oren independently lists driving, taking a later train, and meeting remotely: **generates several options independently**.
   - A coder identifies three alternatives that Oren never mentions considering: those alternatives are not part of the coded option set.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Equating the number of options with decision quality; confusing option generation with comparison; or treating actions discovered only after the initial choice as pre-choice alternatives.

---

### OBS007 — Choice procedure

1. **Name:** Choice procedure.
2. **Operational definition:** The observable rule or sequence used to select among recognized alternatives.
3. **Include when:** At least two alternatives or a default-versus-search choice is established, and the narrative shows how one alternative was selected.
4. **Do not include when:** Only the content of the preferred outcome is known; the coder infers a procedure from the final result; or the narrator gives a trait label such as “I am logical.”
5. **Possible values:** Selects the first acceptable option; compares alternatives on stated dimensions; eliminates options using a non-negotiable constraint; follows a rule, prior commitment, or default; defers selection to another person; chooses the most immediately available option; uses a reversible trial; commits incrementally; combines options rather than selecting one.
6. **Required evidence:** The relevant options, the selection sequence or rule, and the resulting choice.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable episodes using a different procedure. Different decision content does not itself constitute counterevidence if the same procedure was used.
8. **Context modifiers:** Number of options, stakes, reversibility, deadline, decision ownership, expertise, social authority, and whether a minimum threshold was explicit.
9. **Examples:**
   - Priya rejects every apartment outside the transit boundary, then chooses among the remaining two: **eliminates using a non-negotiable constraint**.
   - “I picked the cheapest one” does not establish whether she compared all options, applied a fixed rule, or selected the first acceptable price unless the sequence is described.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Coding decision content as decision procedure; ranking procedures as more or less rational; or assuming that a fast choice lacked comparison.

### OBS008 — Revision after an initial decision

1. **Name:** Revision after an initial decision.
2. **Operational definition:** What happens to a previously selected course of action when new information, changed conditions, social pressure, or further reconsideration occurs.
3. **Include when:** An initial choice or commitment is identifiable and a later event creates an opportunity to maintain, revise, reopen, or abandon it.
4. **Do not include when:** The earlier statement was only a possibility; no initial commitment existed; or the person merely implemented the original choice in stages.
5. **Possible values:** Maintains the decision; revises after new evidence; revises after changed constraints; revises following another person’s request or pressure; explicitly reopens without new external information; oscillates between alternatives; treats the first decision as provisional and later finalizes it; abandons the decision without replacement.
6. **Required evidence:** Initial decision, later input or reconsideration point, temporal ordering, and the final or latest reported action.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes in which similar new input does not produce revision, or revision occurs without the previously coded condition. Record both rather than calling the person generally changeable or fixed.
8. **Context modifiers:** Reversibility, sunk resources, public commitment, authority pressure, relationship, decision stakes, time elapsed, and quality of new evidence as perceived by the narrator.
9. **Examples:**
   - After booking a train, Eli learns the line will close and books a bus: **revises after changed constraints**.
   - Eli says, “I might take the train,” and later chooses a bus: there was no sufficiently definite initial decision to code revision.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating all changed behavior as inconsistency; inferring susceptibility or stubbornness; or failing to distinguish a provisional choice from a committed one.

### OBS009 — Preparation and contingency setup

1. **Name:** Preparation and contingency setup.
2. **Operational definition:** Concrete actions taken before a focal action to arrange materials, sequence steps, rehearse, test, or prepare for foreseeable disruptions.
3. **Include when:** A future action is identified and the person performs specific preparatory behavior related to carrying it out.
4. **Do not include when:** The narrative reports only thinking, worrying, hoping, or intending; the preparation occurs after the focal action begins; or general neatness is described without a target action.
5. **Possible values:** Starts with no reported preparation despite an explicit preparation opportunity; basic material preparation; orders or schedules steps; rehearses or tests; prepares one or more contingencies; prepositions resources; uses a prior template; continues preparing without transitioning to the focal action during the observed period.
6. **Required evidence:** A focal future action, at least one concrete preparatory act or an explicit decision to proceed without available preparation, and correct temporal ordering.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable actions with materially different preparation depth. Unanticipated events do not by themselves contradict contingency preparation unless they were among the anticipated disruptions.
8. **Context modifiers:** Stakes, novelty, complexity, prior failures, deadline, resource availability, reversibility, and whether preparation was required by an institution.
9. **Examples:**
   - Before a presentation, Talia tests the projector, prints a backup, and saves an offline copy: **rehearsal/test plus contingencies**.
   - Talia reports feeling worried for two days but describes no preparation: do not code worry as contingency setup.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Equating amount of preparation with quality; treating extensive preparation as delay without evidence; or counting routine task execution as pre-action preparation.

### OBS010 — Action initiation trigger

1. **Name:** Action initiation trigger.
2. **Operational definition:** The proximal event or condition after which the person begins an identifiable action.
3. **Include when:** The action has a discernible start and the narrative identifies the event, cue, schedule, threshold, or social condition immediately associated with that start.
4. **Do not include when:** Only a broad motive is provided; the trigger is inferred by the coder; or the episode concerns how long the person delayed rather than what eventually prompted the start.
5. **Possible values:** Begins immediately upon deciding; begins at a self-set time; begins at an external deadline or request; begins after an environmental cue; begins when a stated threshold or condition is met; begins as part of a routine sequence; begins with another person present or participating; begins after a strong emotional event; begins after consequences accumulate.
6. **Required evidence:** A bounded start point and a reported or directly sequenced proximal trigger.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable episodes beginning under different triggers. A self-set schedule in one domain and external prompting in another should be coded as context-dependent rather than reduced to one dominant trigger.
8. **Context modifiers:** Voluntariness, deadline, social accountability, task familiarity, emotion, accumulated costs, time of day, and availability of a routine.
9. **Examples:**
   - A calendar alert sounds at 9:00, and Ahmed opens the application and begins: **self-set time or environmental cue**, depending on how the alert was established.
   - “I finally felt motivated” without an identifiable event or start sequence does not establish an initiation trigger.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring internal motivation; confusing the distant reason for a project with its proximal start trigger; or selecting only one trigger when a sequence contains several.

### OBS011 — Transition from stated intention to action

1. **Name:** Transition from stated intention to action.
2. **Operational definition:** The timing and form of actual action after the person has expressed or formed a definite intention.
3. **Include when:** A specific intention precedes a feasible action window and the episode reports whether, when, and how action begins.
4. **Do not include when:** The statement is a wish, aspiration, or vague possibility; no feasible opportunity occurs; or the intention is inferred only from later behavior.
5. **Possible values:** Starts at the first feasible opportunity; starts after a planned delay; starts after repeated unplanned delay; makes a partial or symbolic start; starts only after an external prompt or consequence; does not start during a defined feasible period; is prevented by an external constraint; changes the intention before the opportunity arrives.
6. **Required evidence:** A specific prior intention, a reasonably defined time or opportunity, feasibility information, and subsequent action or explicit non-action.
7. **Counterevidence:** Other comparable intentions with different transitions. External blockage qualifies apparent non-action and must not be coded as unplanned delay.
8. **Context modifiers:** Self-set versus externally imposed intention, deadline, task ambiguity, required coordination, fatigue, competing demands, public commitment, and consequence of delay.
9. **Examples:**
   - On Sunday, Bea decides to submit the form Monday morning and does so at 9:15: **starts after a planned delay**.
   - “I want to learn a language someday” provides no specific action window and cannot support a transition code.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating aspiration as intention; judging sincerity from non-action; or ignoring external barriers that made action infeasible.

---

### OBS012 — Handling simultaneous demands

1. **Name:** Handling simultaneous demands.
2. **Operational definition:** The action sequence used when two or more demands compete for the same limited time, attention, or resource.
3. **Include when:** The demands overlap in time or resource requirements and the person’s allocation or sequencing behavior is reported.
4. **Do not include when:** Tasks occur at separate times; the coder assumes they competed; or only a general statement such as “I prioritize well” is available.
5. **Possible values:** Finishes the current demand before switching; switches immediately to the newly salient demand; assigns separate blocks or a sequence; alternates or multitasks; delegates one or more demands; renegotiates timing or scope; drops one or more demands; takes no action on any demand during the observed window.
6. **Required evidence:** At least two genuinely competing demands, the constrained resource, and the person’s concrete allocation behavior.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable conflicts handled differently. A different sequence under emergency conditions qualifies rather than erases a routine pattern.
8. **Context modifiers:** Urgency, consequence, interpersonal obligation, deadline, task duration, delegability, interruption source, authority, and prior commitment.
9. **Examples:**
   - While finishing a client call, June receives a nonurgent message and schedules it for later: **finishes current demand before switching**.
   - Two projects mentioned in different months are not simultaneous demands unless the narrative establishes overlap.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring the person’s priority rule from task importance as judged by the coder; calling all switching distraction; or ignoring renegotiation as an allocation behavior.

---

### OBS013 — Use of external structure

1. **Name:** Use of external structure.
2. **Operational definition:** Concrete use or modification of reminders, schedules, environments, other people, or access conditions to make a target behavior more or less likely.
3. **Include when:** The person creates, adopts, changes, disables, or deliberately declines an external structure linked to a specific behavior.
4. **Do not include when:** A structured environment merely exists; the link to the target behavior is absent; or the narrative reports only an internal resolution.
5. **Possible values:** Creates reminders or schedules; arranges materials or location; removes or limits access to a competing option; enlists accountability or accompaniment; uses an externally imposed system; modifies a system to fit the task; disables or bypasses a structure; explicitly chooses not to use an available structure.
6. **Required evidence:** Identification of the target behavior, the external structure, and what the person did with that structure.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes in which a similar structure is available but not used, or in which the person acts without it. Structure dependence should not be inferred from one supported episode.
8. **Context modifiers:** Task regularity, digital or physical setting, privacy, available technology, social support, prior missed actions, and whether the structure was voluntary.
9. **Examples:**
   - To remember medication, Noel places it beside the kettle and sets an alarm: **arranges environment and creates reminder**.
   - A tidy desk, without a reported connection to any target action, is not evidence of external structuring.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling the behavior discipline or organization; assuming a tool was used because it was available; or coding another person’s control as self-created structure.

## Continuing, correcting, and completing

### OBS014 — Immediate response to an obstruction

1. **Name:** Immediate response to an obstruction.
2. **Operational definition:** The first observable action after a specific barrier prevents or materially impedes an intended action.
3. **Include when:** A goal-directed action is underway or about to occur, a concrete obstruction appears, and the next response is reported.
4. **Do not include when:** The person changes goals without encountering a barrier; the difficulty is only emotional discomfort with no obstructed action; or the account skips directly to the final outcome.
5. **Possible values:** Repeats the same attempt; inspects or diagnoses the problem; uses a workaround; seeks information or assistance; negotiates or removes the barrier; waits or pauses; reduces or changes the immediate goal; abandons the focal action; redirects effort to another goal.
6. **Required evidence:** The intended action, the obstruction, its effect on the action, and the person’s first subsequent response.
7. **Counterevidence:** Other similar barriers producing different first responses. Later adaptation does not alter the immediate-response value but may be coded under OBS015.
8. **Context modifiers:** Barrier source, controllability, familiarity, time pressure, cost of retrying, access to help, public visibility, and prior encounters with the same barrier.
9. **Examples:**
   - A website rejects Farah’s form, so she first checks every required field: **inspects or diagnoses**.
   - Farah decides before starting that the application is no longer relevant: this is goal revision, not a response to an obstruction.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Coding the eventual resolution instead of the first response; labeling abandonment as lack of persistence; or treating any unpleasant event as an obstruction.

### OBS015 — Strategy adjustment across attempts

1. **Name:** Strategy adjustment across attempts.
2. **Operational definition:** How the person changes or retains a method over successive attempts toward the same or a clearly continuous goal.
3. **Include when:** At least two attempts or an explicit attempt-to-attempt sequence is described.
4. **Do not include when:** Only one attempt is reported; the goal changes completely; or differences between attempts are inferred from different outcomes.
5. **Possible values:** Repeats the method unchanged; changes one component; switches to a substantially different method; combines methods; adds time, effort, money, or personnel; simplifies the method or reduces the target; returns to an earlier method; makes no further attempt after the initial result.
6. **Required evidence:** Continuous goal, identifiable first method, result or problem, and the method used or not used in the next attempt.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later attempts that reverse the apparent pattern. A single method change followed by repeated unchanged attempts should be represented as a sequence, not simply “changes strategy.”
8. **Context modifiers:** Feedback clarity, resource availability, time, stakes, number of failures, assistance, method familiarity, and reversibility.
9. **Examples:**
   - After two cakes fail at the same temperature, Sori lowers the temperature on the third attempt: **changes one component**.
   - One unsuccessful cake with no later attempt is insufficient evidence for strategy adjustment.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling any repeated attempt persistence; treating increased effort as a new method; or using success to infer that the strategy was appropriately adjusted.

### OBS016 — Outcome monitoring and feedback use

1. **Name:** Outcome monitoring and feedback use.
2. **Operational definition:** How the person obtains or receives information about the effects or quality of an action and what behavior follows that information.
3. **Include when:** A result, consequence, or feedback message is available and the narrative identifies either how it was checked or how the person responded.
4. **Do not include when:** Only praise, criticism, or an outcome is mentioned with no subsequent behavior; the person could not reasonably observe the result; or the coder infers receptiveness from politeness.
5. **Possible values:**\
   **Feedback source:** monitors during action; checks the result afterward; solicits feedback; receives unsolicited feedback; learns through a direct consequence.\
   **Response:** adjusts immediately; verifies or tests before adjusting; retains the method after checking; postpones a change; challenges or rejects the feedback; avoids or closes the feedback channel; subsequent response is unknown.
6. **Required evidence:** The feedback source or outcome, its timing, and—when coding use—the action that followed. Source and response may be coded separately when one is unknown.
7. **Counterevidence:** Feedback episodes producing different responses, especially under comparable credibility and stakes. Verbal agreement followed by unchanged behavior qualifies an “adjusts” claim.
8. **Context modifiers:** Feedback source, specificity, timing, relationship, authority, public versus private delivery, reversibility, stakes, and prior experience with the source.
9. **Examples:**
   - Ken asks a colleague to review a draft, receives two specific comments, and revises both sections: **solicited feedback → adjusts**.
   - Ken says “thanks” after a comment, but the interview gives no later action: source is observed; use is **insufficient evidence**.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Coding stated acceptance as behavioral adoption; treating negative outcomes as feedback the person necessarily noticed; or rating the feedback as objectively correct.

---

### OBS017 — Response to a recognized error

1. **Name:** Response to a recognized error.
2. **Operational definition:** The person’s actions after identifying or being informed of a specific possible mistake attributable to their prior action or omission.
3. **Include when:** The possible error, the person’s awareness, and at least one subsequent response are reported.
4. **Do not include when:** The alleged error remains unknown to the person; the event is merely an unfavorable outcome; or the coder decides that a disputed action was erroneous without narrative support.
5. **Possible values:** Identifies the error independently; acknowledges it when informed; verifies before accepting it; corrects the original work; mitigates downstream effects; informs affected people; records or installs a prevention step; disputes or denies the error; withholds or conceals it; takes no corrective action during the defined opportunity.
6. **Required evidence:** A specific possible error, evidence of awareness, and a behavioral response. When validity is disputed, code the verification or dispute behavior without deciding who is right.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later acknowledgment after an initial denial, correction without disclosure, or disclosure without correction. Preserve the full sequence.
8. **Context modifiers:** Error consequence, reversibility, public visibility, authority involvement, blame exposure, available correction, time pressure, and whether others were affected.
9. **Examples:**
   - Lio finds that he sent the wrong file, alerts the recipient, sends the correct one, and changes the filename system: **self-identifies → informs → corrects → prevention step**.
   - Lio disputes an alleged calculation error and produces the original data: code **verifies/disputes**, not concealment or denial as an evaluation.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating disagreement as evidence of dishonesty; collapsing disclosure, correction, and prevention into one value; or judging the seriousness of the person rather than coding actions.

### OBS018 — Completion and follow-up

1. **Name:** Completion and follow-up.
2. **Operational definition:** How the person handles a task or commitment when it reaches, approaches, or fails to reach a defined endpoint.
3. **Include when:** The episode has a recognizable completion criterion or negotiated endpoint.
4. **Do not include when:** The activity is inherently ongoing with no bounded endpoint; the coder imposes a completion standard not present in the situation; or the narrative ends before the endpoint becomes relevant.
5. **Possible values:** Completes the defined endpoint; completes and verifies receipt or effect; hands off and confirms transfer; renegotiates and completes a reduced or changed endpoint; leaves a known element open; stops without closure; returns later to close the task; establishes recurring maintenance rather than one-time closure.
6. **Required evidence:** The endpoint, what remained to be done, the person’s terminal or follow-up action, and enough timing to distinguish delay from noncompletion.
7. **Counterevidence:** Open elements, unconfirmed handoffs, later returns, or changed endpoint agreements. A successful outcome does not prove the person completed every agreed step.
8. **Context modifiers:** Endpoint clarity, dependence on others, deadline, task duration, visibility, handoff requirements, recurring versus one-time work, and changed circumstances.
9. **Examples:**
   - After filing a form, Ada checks for the confirmation receipt and stores it: **completes and verifies**.
   - Daily care for a relative has no single natural endpoint; do not code it as “never completes” without a bounded task.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Confusing stopping with completion; imposing perfection as the endpoint; or ignoring negotiated changes to the original task.

### OBS019 — Repetition under recurring conditions

1. **Name:** Repetition under recurring conditions.
2. **Operational definition:** Recurrence or nonrecurrence of a specific behavior when a sufficiently similar cue, situation, or opportunity appears more than once.
3. **Include when:** At least two occurrences are described, or an anchored repeated-series report specifies the recurring condition and behavior.
4. **Do not include when:** Only one episode is available; the repeated behavior occurs in materially different situations with no shared opportunity; or repetition is inferred from a global identity statement.
5. **Possible values:** Consistent recurrence; intermittent recurrence; recurrence only under a documented subset of conditions; substitution of a different recurring behavior; disruption when a modifier appears; deliberate interruption or cessation; recurrence maintained by an external structure.
6. **Required evidence:** Repeated comparable opportunities, the behavior at each opportunity or a sufficiently anchored series account, and any known exceptions.
7. **Counterevidence:** Occasions on which the recurring condition appears but the behavior does not, or the same behavior appears without the proposed condition.
8. **Context modifiers:** Frequency of opportunity, setting, time, social presence, reminders, fatigue, travel, resource availability, and interruptions to routine.
9. **Examples:**
   - Each time an invoice arrives, Mei enters it before the end of that day across four reported months: **consistent recurrence**.
   - Mei entered one invoice immediately: that episode is observed, but recurrence remains **insufficient evidence**.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling recurrence a habit or automaticity without evidence; ignoring missed opportunities; or treating repeated outcomes as repeated behavior.

### OBS020 — Change after prior experience

1. **Name:** Change after prior experience.
2. **Operational definition:** Whether and how a prior outcome or episode is explicitly used in a later comparable action.
3. **Include when:** A prior experience and a later opportunity are both described, and the narrator explicitly links them or gives a sequence that directly establishes use of the earlier experience.
4. **Do not include when:** The later behavior merely differs; the coder assumes the earlier event caused the change; or the later situation is not sufficiently comparable.
5. **Possible values:** Repeats the prior approach; changes behavior only in the same setting; applies the change to similar settings; applies the change across different settings; changes temporarily and later returns to the earlier behavior; changes only after repeated prior outcomes; recalls the outcome but reports no later behavioral change.
6. **Required evidence:** Time-ordered episodes, comparable opportunity, prior outcome, later action, and an explicit or directly supported link between them.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later episodes in which the same prior lesson is remembered but not used, or the altered behavior predates the cited experience.
8. **Context modifiers:** Similarity of later context, time elapsed, outcome severity, number of prior occurrences, availability of alternatives, and whether the original condition persisted.
9. **Examples:**
   - After missing one flight, Nuru says that on the next two trips he left home an hour earlier because of it: **change in similar settings**.
   - Nuru later leaves earlier, but no connection to the missed flight is stated and another schedule change intervened: prior-experience use is insufficient.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring learning from any behavioral difference; treating a stated lesson as enacted change; or overlooking later reversion.

## Competing influences and state-linked shifts

### OBS021 — Response to a salient competing option

1. **Name:** Response to a salient competing option.
2. **Operational definition:** What the person does when an alternative action becomes salient while another intended or ongoing course of action is available.
3. **Include when:** The original course, the noticed alternative, and the behavioral response to their competition are all reported.
4. **Do not include when:** The alternative was never noticed; the original activity had already ended; or the coder labels something a temptation or distraction without evidence that the person experienced it as competing.
5. **Possible values:** Continues the original course; switches fully to the alternative; alternates between them; schedules the alternative for later; reduces access or exposure to the alternative; seeks external restraint or accountability; compares and deliberately selects one; abandons both during the observed period.
6. **Required evidence:** An existing plan or action, awareness of the alternative, temporal overlap, and the resulting behavior.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable competing options handled differently. Differences by importance or social obligation should be retained as modifiers.
8. **Context modifiers:** Attractiveness as reported, urgency, social invitation, ease of switching, fatigue, deadline, visibility, reversibility, and external controls.
9. **Examples:**
   - While writing a report, Jules receives an invitation and places it on the calendar for after the deadline: **schedules the alternative later**.
   - An invitation was sent but Jules never saw it: there was no salient competing option for this code.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Moralizing the alternative; inferring weak control from switching; or coding every interruption as a chosen competing option.

### OBS022 — Emotion-linked action shift

1. **Name:** Emotion-linked action shift.
2. **Operational definition:** A change, delay, intensification, reduction, or maintenance of behavior that the person explicitly connects to a reported emotional state, or that occurs in a directly described emotion-to-action sequence.
3. **Include when:** The emotional experience is reported by the person and its temporal or stated connection to action is clear.
4. **Do not include when:** Emotion is inferred from tone, outcome, facial expression, or the coder’s interpretation; or emotion is reported with no action information.
5. **Possible values:** Takes an immediate unplanned action; communicates directly with the apparent source; withdraws or reduces engagement; delays a decision or response; seeks contact or support; changes location or engages in a self-directed activity; increases speed, force, or amount of action; decreases pace or scope; maintains the previously planned behavior despite the emotion.
6. **Required evidence:** A named or clearly described felt state, action before or intended action where relevant, subsequent behavior, and a supported connection.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes involving a similar reported emotion but a different action pattern. Different emotion intensity or relationship context may qualify the comparison.
8. **Context modifiers:** Emotion named by narrator, intensity, duration, relationship, public/private setting, immediate threat, fatigue, available pause, and social support.
9. **Examples:**
   - Angry after receiving a message, Sami drafts a reply but waits until morning before sending it: **delays response following emotion**.
   - The transcript contains capital letters, and the coder infers anger: no emotion-linked code is permitted without narrative evidence.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Diagnosing emotional regulation; assuming an emotion caused action because it preceded it; or treating continued action as absence of emotion.

### OBS023 — Recovery and re-engagement after disruption

1. **Name:** Recovery and re-engagement after disruption.
2. **Operational definition:** Whether, when, and how the person resumes, modifies, or replaces an interrupted activity after an emotional event, setback, conflict, illness episode, or other disruption.
3. **Include when:** A focal activity is interrupted or materially reduced and a later observation window establishes what happens next.
4. **Do not include when:** No prior activity existed; the interview ends immediately after the disruption; or the later action is unrelated and no replacement relationship is stated.
5. **Possible values:** Continues immediately; pauses deliberately and returns; returns after external prompting or support; returns in a reduced or modified form; shows repeated stop–start re-entry; substitutes another route to the same endpoint; switches to a different goal; does not return within the defined observed period.
6. **Required evidence:** Pre-disruption activity, the disruption, change or cessation, a meaningful follow-up period, and later behavior.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later re-engagement after an apparent nonreturn, or episodes with different recovery paths. The observation window must be stated.
8. **Context modifiers:** Disruption type and duration, physical recovery, social support, task deadline, availability of substitute routes, prior investment, and whether the environment changed.
9. **Examples:**
   - After an argument interrupts Leila’s studying, she calls a friend, sleeps, and resumes the next morning: **returns after external support and a pause**.
   - The account ends at the moment Leila stops studying: “does not return” is not justified.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling any pause failure to recover; ignoring the length of the observation window; or treating a substitute route as abandonment.

### OBS024 — Load- or fatigue-linked behavior change

1. **Name:** Load- or fatigue-linked behavior change.
2. **Operational definition:** A documented difference in behavior associated with reported fatigue, pain, illness, sleep loss, or unusually high concurrent workload.
3. **Include when:** The relevant state or load is directly reported and behavior can be compared with a lower-load baseline or with an earlier phase of the same episode.
4. **Do not include when:** Fatigue is inferred from a mistake or slow action; no comparison is available; or the physical condition is reported without behavioral consequences.
5. **Possible values:** Reduces pace or amount; simplifies the method; increases checking or external support; postpones action; stops action; preserves one task by dropping others; makes more reported errors or omissions; shows no demonstrated behavioral change despite the state; varies by task or context.
6. **Required evidence:** Reported load or condition, a defined behavior, and paired or within-episode comparison sufficient to identify change or demonstrated maintenance.
7. **Counterevidence:** High-load episodes without the coded change, or low-load episodes showing the same behavior. These may show that the behavior is not specific to fatigue.
8. **Context modifiers:** Sleep, pain, illness, workload type, duration, time of day, task familiarity, stakes, deadlines, available rest, and support.
9. **Examples:**
   - After a night shift, Jae uses a checklist and postpones two nonurgent tasks: **increases support and postpones under fatigue**.
   - Jae makes a typo, and the coder assumes tiredness: do not include without reported fatigue and comparison.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating fatigue as a diagnosis; assuming causality from coincidence; or coding “no change” merely because no difference was mentioned.

### OBS025 — Pressure-linked behavior change

1. **Name:** Pressure-linked behavior change.
2. **Operational definition:** A documented change or demonstrated maintenance of decision or action behavior as time pressure, stakes, scrutiny, threat of consequence, or interpersonal pressure increases.
3. **Include when:** Pressure is identifiable and there is a baseline, explicit comparison, or within-episode change as pressure rises.
4. **Do not include when:** The situation is merely important; the coder infers pressure; or only one behavior under one pressure level is known.
5. **Possible values:** Acts faster or shortens the process; narrows the option set; increases checking or preparation; delays or ceases action; transfers or seeks authorization; becomes more direct in communication; withdraws or reduces engagement; retains the same documented process; shows different changes under different forms of pressure.
6. **Required evidence:** Type of pressure, behavior under pressure, and a valid comparison with lower pressure or an earlier phase.
7. **Counterevidence:** Similar pressure without the coded change, or the same behavior when pressure is absent. Separate time pressure, social pressure, and high stakes when they have different effects.
8. **Context modifiers:** Pressure source, deadline proximity, public scrutiny, authority, consequence severity, reversibility, familiarity, social support, and fatigue.
9. **Examples:**
   - Under a two-hour deadline, Amadou compares two vendors rather than the ten he usually reviews: **narrows options under time pressure**.
   - Amadou compares two vendors in one urgent episode, but no usual comparison process is known: pressure-linked change is insufficient.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Attributing all urgent behavior to pressure; combining distinct pressure sources; or treating faster action as better or worse performance.

### OBS026 — Intention–action correspondence

1. **Name:** Intention–action correspondence.
2. **Operational definition:** The relationship between a definite stated intention and the person’s later behavior during a relevant opportunity.
3. **Include when:** The intention precedes the action opportunity and both are specific enough to compare.
4. **Do not include when:** The statement is a preference, aspiration, prediction, or post hoc explanation; the action opportunity never occurs; or the person changes the intention before the opportunity.
5. **Possible values:** Enacts the stated intention; enacts it after a planned delay; enacts it after an unplanned delay; enacts it partially; performs a substitute action toward the same stated endpoint; performs an incompatible or opposite action; takes no action despite a feasible opportunity; is prevented by an external constraint; explicitly revises the intention before acting.
6. **Required evidence:** Prior intention, opportunity and feasibility, time window, actual behavior, and external constraints where relevant.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes in which similar intentions have different enactment. A single mismatch contradicts an “always” claim but produces **mixed** evidence if other comparable intentions are enacted.
8. **Context modifiers:** Public versus private intention, specificity, deadline, external dependence, social commitment, fatigue, competing demands, and changed information.
9. **Examples:**
   - On Thursday, Arun says he will call on Friday and calls Friday afternoon: **enacts the stated intention**.
   - Arun intends to visit, but a storm closes the only road: **prevented by external constraint**, not intention–action mismatch.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring insincerity; counting vague wishes as intentions; or ignoring that a substitute action may pursue the same stated endpoint.

## Social interaction and coordination

### OBS027 — Help seeking and use of help

1. **Name:** Help seeking and use of help.
2. **Operational definition:** How the person requests, signals for, accepts, declines, or uses assistance when facing a task, obstacle, or need.
3. **Include when:** A concrete need or obstacle exists and behavior concerning a potential helper or offered assistance is reported.
4. **Do not include when:** The person only seeks factual information; no potential source of assistance exists; assistance is imposed without a relevant need; or the coder assumes help was necessary.
5. **Possible values:** Requests help early; attempts independently before requesting; requests after prompting; signals indirectly; waits for an offer; delegates immediately; accepts and uses offered help; accepts but does not use it; declines offered help; seeks help from several sources; no request despite an explicit feasible opportunity.
6. **Required evidence:** The focal need, an identifiable potential or actual helper, timing of the request or offer, and what the person did with the assistance.
7. **Counterevidence:** Similar needs handled independently, earlier, later, or through different helpers. Lack of help seeking is not contradictory when no suitable helper was available.
8. **Context modifiers:** Task type, expertise gap, relationship, power difference, privacy, cost, urgency, prior response from helpers, and whether help created obligation.
9. **Examples:**
   - After two unsuccessful attempts, Rosa asks a colleague to demonstrate the procedure and then uses it: **tries independently → requests → uses help**.
   - Advice is offered about a task Rosa does not need to perform: this is not a relevant help-seeking opportunity.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Equating help seeking with dependence; confusing information search with practical assistance; or coding nonrequest without establishing an available helper.

### OBS028 — Initiating social contact or coordination

1. **Name:** Initiating social contact or coordination.
2. **Operational definition:** Who begins a needed or optional interaction and how the person initiates or waits for coordination.
3. **Include when:** A concrete interaction or joint-action opportunity exists and the narrative establishes who made the first coordinating move.
4. **Do not include when:** Only general sociability is described; contact occurs accidentally; or the person responds within an interaction that was already initiated by someone else.
5. **Possible values:** Initiates directly; initiates through a message, system, or intermediary; proposes a plan or role division; co-initiates after a minimal cue; responds but does not initiate; initiates only after a role assignment, deadline, or prompting; waits for the other party; makes no contact during a feasible defined window.
6. **Required evidence:** The coordination opportunity, relevant parties, who acted first, the form of initiation or waiting, and feasibility.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable interactions with a different initiator pattern. Mandatory role initiation should be distinguished from optional initiation.
8. **Context modifiers:** Relationship, role, hierarchy, familiarity, public/private channel, prior contact, expected norms, uncertainty about reception, and urgency.
9. **Examples:**
   - Before a joint assignment begins, Omar sends the first message and proposes who will draft each section: **initiates and proposes roles**.
   - Omar receives an invitation and promptly answers: this is responsive engagement, not initiation of that interaction.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating initiation as extraversion or leadership; ignoring institutional responsibility; or coding responsiveness as initiation.

### OBS029 — Communicating needs, limits, or uncertainty

1. **Name:** Communicating needs, limits, or uncertainty.
2. **Operational definition:** How and when the person communicates a personally experienced need, capacity limit, constraint, lack of knowledge, or uncertainty that is relevant to shared action.
3. **Include when:** The person reports the need, limit, or uncertainty and has a meaningful opportunity to communicate it.
4. **Do not include when:** The coder infers the need from appearance or behavior; the information is irrelevant to others; or the episode concerns responding to someone else’s request rather than communicating one’s own condition.
5. **Possible values:** Communicates directly before action; communicates directly after a problem appears; communicates indirectly or by hint; discloses only selected parts; uses writing or an intermediary; withholds despite a relevant feasible opportunity; communicates only after a consequence occurs; corrects or revises an earlier statement.
6. **Required evidence:** The experienced need, limit, or uncertainty; its relevance to the interaction; an opportunity to communicate; and the actual communication or explicit withholding.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable situations in which the person communicates earlier, later, more directly, or not at all. Different privacy or power conditions should qualify the comparison.
8. **Context modifiers:** Relationship, power asymmetry, anticipated consequence as reported, privacy, stigma as locally described, urgency, role expectations, and availability of a private channel.
9. **Examples:**
   - Before a trip, Hana tells the group that she cannot drive safely at night: **direct communication before action**.
   - Hana looks tired, but neither she nor the narrative reports a need for rest: the coder may not infer an uncommunicated need.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring internal needs; judging disclosure as healthy or unhealthy; or treating selective communication as deception without evidence.

### OBS030 — Response to requests and stated limits

1. **Name:** Response to requests and stated limits.
2. **Operational definition:** The person’s behavior after another party makes a concrete request or states a boundary, refusal, capacity limit, or condition.
3. **Include when:** The request or limit and the person’s subsequent response are identifiable.
4. **Do not include when:** The other person merely expresses a preference; no response opportunity occurs; or the episode concerns the person stating their own limit.
5. **Possible values:** Accepts as requested; asks for clarification; negotiates scope, timing, or conditions; declines directly; defers or does not respond during a feasible window; complies initially and later renegotiates or withdraws; seeks an exception; adapts to the other person’s stated limit; involves a third party; bypasses or acts contrary to the stated limit.
6. **Required evidence:** The content of the request or limit, evidence that the person received or understood it, the feasible response window, and actual behavior.
7. **Counterevidence:** Similar requests or limits receiving different responses. A response under authority pressure should not be generalized to optional peer requests.
8. **Context modifiers:** Requester relationship and authority, cost, urgency, clarity, prior agreement, ability to decline, public/private setting, and consequence of refusal.
9. **Examples:**
   - Asked to work an extra shift, Musa agrees to half the shift after discussing childcare: **negotiates scope**.
   - A colleague casually says that mornings are preferable but makes no request or limit: do not code unless it functions as a concrete request in context.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Equating direct decline with conflict; treating compliance as agreement; or judging whether the other person’s limit was reasonable.

### OBS031 — Behavior during disagreement

1. **Name:** Behavior during disagreement.
2. **Operational definition:** The person’s observable communication and action after recognizing that another party holds an incompatible position, interpretation, plan, or demand.
3. **Include when:** A concrete disagreement is established and at least one response behavior is reported.
4. **Do not include when:** The person is merely disappointed; the parties unknowingly act differently; or conflict is inferred from silence without supporting context.
5. **Possible values:** Asks questions or restates the other position; states their own position; supplies reasons or evidence; proposes a compromise, trial, or decision rule; concedes; postpones discussion; withdraws or ends the exchange; involves a third party; repeats a position without engaging the other’s content; uses personal criticism, threat, raised voice, or another specifically reported escalation behavior.
6. **Required evidence:** The points of disagreement, awareness by the person, and observable response. Multiple values should be recorded in sequence.
7. **Counterevidence:** Other disagreements involving a different sequence or outcome. A calm disagreement in one power relationship does not erase withdrawal in another.
8. **Context modifiers:** Relationship, hierarchy, public/private setting, stakes, time pressure, repeated history, perceived safety, medium of communication, and third-party presence.
9. **Examples:**
   - When a teammate rejects her plan, Zuri asks for the objection, restates it, and proposes a one-week test: **clarifies → proposes a trial**.
   - The call goes silent because the connection fails: do not code withdrawal from disagreement.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Applying labels such as assertive, passive, or aggressive; inferring tone not reported; or coding the resolution rather than the person’s behavior.

### OBS032 — Interpersonal repair after strain or harm

1. **Name:** Interpersonal repair after strain or harm.
2. **Operational definition:** Actions taken after a person recognizes relational strain, offense, broken trust, or harm and has an opportunity to address the relationship or consequence.
3. **Include when:** A rupture or perceived harm is described, the person is aware of it, and later relational behavior is reported.
4. **Do not include when:** No rupture is recognized; the person merely feels regret without action; or normal interaction resumes but the observation window is too short to determine whether repair occurred.
5. **Possible values:** Initiates acknowledgment; apologizes; explains or clarifies; offers restitution or corrective action; invites discussion; changes later behavior; resumes contact without discussing the rupture; waits for or responds to the other person’s repair attempt; uses an intermediary; withdraws or ends contact; makes a repair attempt that is rejected.
6. **Required evidence:** The strain or harm, awareness, a feasible repair opportunity, and the subsequent action or explicit non-action.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later repair after initial withdrawal, repeated harm after a promised change, or repair actions in some relationships but not others. Preserve sequences and outcomes separately.
8. **Context modifiers:** Relationship closeness, severity, responsibility as perceived by the narrator, public/private setting, safety, power, time elapsed, and the other person’s response.
9. **Examples:**
   - After damaging a borrowed item, Celia contacts the owner, apologizes, and pays for replacement: **initiates acknowledgment → apology → restitution**.
   - Conversation later returns to ordinary topics with no discussion: code **resumes contact without discussion**, not apology or forgiveness.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Inferring sincerity; equating explanation with excuse; treating acceptance by the other person as necessary for a repair attempt to be coded.

### OBS033 — Follow-through on interpersonal commitments

1. **Name:** Follow-through on interpersonal commitments.
2. **Operational definition:** What the person does after making a specific commitment on which another person or group may rely.
3. **Include when:** The commitment, expected action, recipient, and relevant timing or condition are sufficiently concrete.
4. **Do not include when:** The statement is “I will try,” a vague intention, a social courtesy with no reliance, or a commitment imposed without the person’s assent.
5. **Possible values:** Fulfills as agreed; fulfills before or after the agreed time; renegotiates before the due point; renegotiates after the due point; fulfills partially; delegates with the other party’s agreement; fails to fulfill and informs the other party; fails to fulfill without communication during the defined period; withdraws before reliance occurs; counterpart changes the terms.
6. **Required evidence:** Specific commitment, evidence of assent, expected timing or condition, actual performance, and communication about changes.
7. **Counterevidence:** Comparable commitments with different follow-through. A missed commitment caused by an external block should retain the block and any communication behavior rather than be treated as simple nonfulfillment.
8. **Context modifiers:** Relationship, formality, public commitment, deadline, dependence by others, changed circumstances, feasibility, number of simultaneous commitments, and authority.
9. **Examples:**
   - Tariq promises a draft Tuesday, warns the collaborator Monday that it will be late, and they agree on Thursday: **renegotiates before due**.
   - “I’ll see what I can do” without a defined deliverable or assent is insufficient evidence of a commitment.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Treating every intention as a promise; ignoring renegotiation; or rating reliability without counting relevant opportunities and constraints.

## Cross-episode pattern descriptors

### OBS034 — Context-linked variability

1. **Name:** Context-linked variability.
2. **Operational definition:** The degree and form of change in an underlying observable’s substantive value across comparable opportunities that differ in one or more documented contexts.
3. **Include when:** Several episode-level codes for the same observable are available across at least two meaningfully different contexts.
4. **Do not include when:** Only one context is represented; episodes involve noncomparable opportunities; or variability is asserted through global self-description alone.
5. **Possible values:** Same value across sampled contexts; systematic context-specific split; graded or threshold change with a modifier; one documented exception to an otherwise repeated pattern; differing values without an identified context relation; several values with overlapping context conditions.
6. **Required evidence:** Ordinarily at least three qualifying episodes across at least two context classes. A systematic context split should ideally have more than one opportunity in each condition; otherwise mark it provisional or insufficient.
7. **Counterevidence:** Episodes that violate the proposed context rule. Every exception must be retained, including exceptions that occur in the supposedly stable condition.
8. **Context modifiers:** The modifier being evaluated, opportunity comparability, distribution of episodes across conditions, time period, domain, relationship, pressure, fatigue, stakes, and data density.
9. **Examples:**
   - In two work and two family episodes, Beno asks for help only after two independent attempts: **same value across sampled contexts**.
   - Three examples all come from one workplace: cross-context consistency remains **insufficient evidence**.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling a pattern stable because the same value appears repeatedly in one setting; treating an isolated exception as noise; or attributing a context effect without comparable opportunities.

---

### OBS035 — Change in repeated behavior over time

1. **Name:** Change in repeated behavior over time.
2. **Operational definition:** The temporal trajectory of the same underlying observable across time-ordered, sufficiently comparable opportunities.
3. **Include when:** Multiple episodes can be ordered in time and permit comparison of the same behavior under reasonably similar opportunities.
4. **Do not include when:** Only an earlier and later self-description are provided; opportunities differ substantially; or a single unusual episode is treated as a life change.
5. **Possible values:** No demonstrated change over the observed interval; gradual shift; abrupt shift following a named event or changed condition; temporary shift followed by return; alternating or cyclical pattern; isolated deviation followed by prior pattern; different trajectories in different contexts.
6. **Required evidence:** Ordinarily at least three time-ordered opportunities. An abrupt event-linked shift requires either an explicit narrator link or a sufficiently close and repeated before–after pattern. The observed time span must be recorded.
7. **Counterevidence:** Later reversion, earlier examples of the supposedly new behavior, or context changes that account for apparent temporal differences. These qualify the trajectory rather than being discarded.
8. **Context modifiers:** Length of observation, life period, environmental change, relationship change, role change, health or workload, opportunity frequency, and whether the coding source compresses long periods.
9. **Examples:**
   - In three episodes before a schedule change, Ema waits for reminders; in four later episodes, she starts from a self-set calendar: **abrupt temporal shift after a named condition change**.
   - One early episode and one late episode differ: that is insufficient to establish a trajectory.
10. **Common coding mistakes:** Calling any difference development or maturation; inferring the cause of change; ignoring reversion; or comparing behavior from noncomparable life circumstances.

### DEVELOPMENT NOTES

#### Constructs considered but rejected as too vague

The following were considered but excluded as primary observables because first-person interview material would not reliably distinguish them without additional assumptions:

- **Motivation:** Different initiation, delay, persistence, or effort patterns can occur with the same reported desire. Replaced by OBS010, OBS011, OBS014, OBS015, and OBS026.
- **Willpower or self-control:** These labels collapse responses to competing options, environmental structuring, fatigue, pressure, and opportunity. Replaced by OBS013, OBS021, OBS024, and OBS025.
- **Resilience:** This can refer to persistence, adaptation, recovery, emotional experience, outcome attainment, or social support. Replaced by OBS014, OBS015, OBS020, and OBS023.
- **Adaptability:** Too broad without specifying what changed, in response to what, and over what interval. Replaced by OBS008, OBS015, OBS016, OBS020, OBS034, and OBS035.
- **Decisiveness:** May refer to decision speed, number of options, commitment stability, or willingness to act under uncertainty. Replaced by OBS004, OBS006, OBS007, OBS008, and OBS011.
- **Impulsivity:** Often inferred from speed or adverse outcomes without establishing available information, intention, emotion, alternatives, or feasibility. Relevant behavior is distributed across OBS003, OBS004, OBS010, OBS021, OBS022, and OBS026.
- **Avoidance as a general trait:** Non-entry, delay, withdrawal, declining, or nonresponse may have different mechanisms and constraints. These are separately represented in OBS001, OBS004, OBS011, OBS021, OBS023, OBS028, OBS030, and OBS031.
- **Confidence:** Reported confidence may be retained as an experience, but it does not uniquely predict entry, information seeking, help seeking, disagreement behavior, or follow-through.
- **Trust:** Trust is difficult to infer uniquely from disclosure, delegation, exposure, help seeking, or acceptance of information. Those actions should be coded directly.
- **Empathy, emotional maturity, emotional intelligence, authenticity, leadership, and good judgment:** These are broad, evaluative, culturally dependent, or interpretation-heavy. Specific communication, coordination, feedback, repair, and decision behaviors are coded instead.
- **Rationality:** Requires an external normative model and would collapse decision procedures with outcomes. The codebook records procedures and consequences without rating rationality.
- **Risk tolerance:** Replaced by the concrete exposure-setting behavior in OBS005, because the same person may cap one kind of loss and accept another.
- **Values or moral character:** Narrated values may be recorded as claims, but behavior–value correspondence would require separately specified situations and opportunities. No moral inference is made from isolated action.
- **Identity consistency:** Global identity claims are retained as claims, not treated as behavioral constructs. OBS026 addresses specific intentions, while OBS034 and OBS035 address episode-level consistency and change.

#### Observable pairs that may still overlap

- **OBS003 and OBS004:** Information seeking occurs before or during uncertainty reduction; OBS004 begins at the point meaningful uncertainty remains unresolved. A single episode may legitimately receive both codes.
- **OBS004 and OBS005:** A limited trial may both permit action under uncertainty and limit possible loss. Both may be coded when the narrative supports both functions without inferring motive.
- **OBS006 and OBS007:** OBS006 concerns how alternatives entered consideration; OBS007 concerns how selection occurred after alternatives were available.
- **OBS008 and OBS020:** OBS008 concerns revision within a decision episode; OBS020 concerns use of one episode in a later episode.
- **OBS009, OBS010, and OBS011:** OBS009 concerns preparation, OBS010 the proximal start trigger, and OBS011 the relation between prior intention and actual starting.
- **OBS012 and OBS021:** OBS012 requires competing demands on shared resources; OBS021 concerns a newly salient alternative to an intended or ongoing course. Some episodes may contain both.
- **OBS013 and OBS021:** Removing access to an alternative is both environmental structuring and a response to a competing option.
- **OBS014 and OBS015:** OBS014 codes the first response to a barrier; OBS015 codes changes or repetition across successive attempts.
- **OBS016 and OBS017:** General outcome feedback belongs in OBS016. A recognized specific error and actions concerning it belong in OBS017. Both may apply.
- **OBS019, OBS034, and OBS035:** OBS019 establishes recurrence under similar conditions. OBS034 describes differences across contexts. OBS035 describes change across time.
- **OBS022, OBS024, and OBS025:** Emotion, fatigue/load, and pressure may co-occur. Each should be coded only when its own required evidence and comparison are present.
- **OBS023 and OBS035:** OBS023 concerns resumption after one disruption; OBS035 concerns a repeated temporal trajectory.
- **OBS027 and OBS003:** Requests for information belong in OBS003; requests for practical, emotional, material, or task assistance belong in OBS027. Mixed requests may receive both codes.
- **OBS029 and OBS030:** OBS029 concerns communicating one’s own need or limit. OBS030 concerns responding to another party’s request or limit.
- **OBS031 and OBS032:** OBS031 covers behavior during disagreement; OBS032 begins after strain or harm has been recognized and repair becomes possible.
- **OBS026 and OBS033:** OBS026 applies to any specific personal intention. OBS033 applies only to an assented commitment on which another party may rely.

#### Areas requiring particular attention in pilot coding

1. **Episode segmentation:** Coders may differ on whether a long narrative is one episode or several linked episodes. Pilot work should establish rules for changes in setting, goal, opportunity, and time.
2. **Opportunity denominators:** Non-action codes will be unreliable unless coders consistently identify awareness, feasibility, and the relevant time window.
3. **Retrospective compression:** Statements such as “I always did that” may omit exceptions, changing conditions, or separate episodes. Anchoring requirements should be tested.
4. **Sequence coding:** Several observables permit multi-step values. Coders need practice preserving order rather than selecting only the final or most salient action.
5. **External constraint versus chosen delay:** This distinction affects OBS001, OBS004, OBS011, OBS018, OBS023, OBS026, OBS028, and OBS033.
6. **Action-guiding cue identification:** Pilot coding should test agreement on what constitutes an explicit enough cue-to-action link for OBS002.
7. **Information versus assistance:** OBS003 and OBS027 may be difficult to distinguish when advice includes both knowledge and practical intervention.
8. **Feedback adoption:** Verbal acknowledgment, agreement, behavioral trial, and sustained behavior change must remain separate.
9. **Comparable contexts:** OBS034 and OBS035 require judgments about whether opportunities are sufficiently similar. A formal comparability checklist may be needed after pilot work.
10. **Mixed versus contradicted:** Coders should specify the scope of the proposition being evaluated. A counterexample to “always” differs from mixed evidence about “usually.”
11. **Cultural and organizational norms:** Directness, timing, commitment language, repair practices, help seeking, and role initiation may carry different local meanings. Coding should retain the actual behavior and locally described expectation without applying a universal norm.
12. **Observation-window effects:** “Did not return,” “did not respond,” “did not complete,” and similar values depend on a defensible time window.
13. **Self-description anchoring:** Pilot coding should monitor whether coders are inadvertently allowing labels such as “organized,” “private,” or “independent” to influence episode codes.
14. **Rare opportunities:** Some observables, particularly error response, interpersonal repair, and exposure to possible loss, may remain not applicable or insufficient for many interviews. Coders must not infer values to fill the matrix.
15. **Coder burden:** Thirty-five observables may be compact relative to the behavior space but still cognitively demanding. Pilot data should determine whether staged coding passes improve reliability without changing definitions.

#### Assumptions made in this draft

- First-person reports are treated as evidence about what the narrator reports doing or experiencing, not as independently verified historical facts.
- The interviews will contain enough concrete chronology to distinguish prior intention, opportunity, action, and outcome in at least some episodes.
- The primary goal is descriptive within-person measurement, not prediction, diagnosis, explanation, or evaluation.
- Not every observable is expected to apply to every person or interview.
- The interview corpus may not provide valid frequency denominators; where denominators are absent, summaries should use episode counts and avoid population-like frequency claims.
- A minimum of two qualifying occurrences is adequate for a provisional recurrence statement, but not for strong claims of cross-context stability or temporal trajectory.
- Different mechanisms may legitimately be coded for the same action. Multi-coding is preferable to forcing one inferred explanation.
- Context modifiers will be recorded in locally meaningful language and will not be treated as universal causal factors.
- No observable is assumed to be beneficial, harmful, mature, deficient, normal, or abnormal.
- No composite score, person type, rank ordering, or normative profile is implied by the codebook.
- Findings apply only to the opportunities represented in the available narrative.
- Later reliability or validity work may justify splitting, combining, narrowing, or retiring observables, but those changes should not be retroactively inserted into this first-pass artifact.
