# Neutral Behavioral Measurement Codebook — Independent Replication Draft v1

## 1. Purpose and scope

This codebook is designed for trained human coders analyzing first-person autobiographical interviews. Its purpose is to describe **recurring patterns in what narrators report perceiving, experiencing, choosing, doing, not doing, repeating, or changing in concrete situations**.

The system is intentionally theory-neutral. It does not organize observations into personality types, psychological traits, diagnoses, demographic categories, biological explanations, spiritual systems, birth-based systems, or any other external explanatory model.

Codes describe behavior at the level of reported episodes. They do not explain why a person behaves that way unless the narrator explicitly reports a reason, and even then the reason is recorded only as a **narrator-stated rationale**, not treated as an established cause.

Administrative code domains below are filing devices only. They are not latent constructs, scales, dimensions, or proposed components of personality.

---

# 2. Core coding principles

## 2.1 Primary evidence unit: the concrete behavioral episode

The primary evidence unit is a **specific autobiographical episode** in which the narrator identifies enough of the following to reconstruct what occurred:

- a situation, event, demand, opportunity, or trigger;
- what the narrator noticed, knew, or experienced;
- a choice point or possible course of action, when relevant;
- what the narrator did or explicitly chose not to do;
- relevant sequence or timing;
- relevant constraints;
- and, when available, the immediate outcome.

Examples of qualifying episode forms include:

> “When my supervisor returned the draft on Tuesday and said the figures were inconsistent, I went back through the spreadsheet that afternoon and changed how I was calculating them.”

> “My sister asked me to drive her to the airport, but I had already promised to attend my son's school event, so I told her I couldn't do it.”

Statements such as “I am very organized,” “I never give up,” “I am bad with conflict,” or “people say I am independent” are **not sufficient behavioral evidence by themselves**. They may be stored as narrator claims but require concrete episodes before they affect behavioral coding.

## 2.2 Recurrence requirement

A concrete episode may be coded after one qualifying occurrence. A **recurring-pattern summary**, however, normally requires at least **two independent concrete episodes** supporting the same substantive value within the stated context.

Retellings of the same event count once.

Two moments in the same continuous incident normally count as one episode unless they contain clearly separate opportunities, decisions, or behavioral cycles.

When only one qualifying episode exists, code the episode but assign the person-level recurrence summary **insufficient evidence**.

Codes with inherently repetitive content, especially NBM-B04 Routine Enactment, use a higher threshold specified in that code.

## 2.3 Context-bounded conclusions

Pattern statements must be scoped to the evidence.

Preferred:

> “In three reported workplace deadline episodes, the narrator began the required task only after an external reminder.”

Not permitted:

> “The narrator is a procrastinator.”

If behavior differs systematically by context, retain the distinction:

> “Observed: early help-seeking in unfamiliar technical tasks. Mixed across personal problems.”

Do not average away exceptions merely to produce a single person-level value.

## 2.4 Non-action rule: Awareness–Opportunity–Feasibility gate

A reported failure to act may count as behavioral evidence only when all three conditions are adequately established:

**Awareness:** The narrator knew about the relevant demand, opportunity, option, or possible action.

**Opportunity:** There was a meaningful occasion or time window in which the action could have been taken.

**Reasonable feasibility:** The narrative establishes that the action was realistically possible given the circumstances reported.

If any component is missing, do not infer non-action from silence.

For example, “I never asked my manager for help” is not evidence of declining to seek help if the interview never establishes that the narrator knew the manager could help, had access to the manager, and reasonably could have asked.

## 2.5 No hidden-cause inference

Coders must not translate behavior into unreported motives.

From:

> “I checked the application five times before submitting it.”

Permitted:

> Repeated outcome/pre-submission checking was reported.

Not permitted:

> The narrator was anxious, perfectionistic, insecure, obsessive, conscientious, or afraid of failure.

If the narrator says, “I kept checking because I was afraid I had entered the wrong number,” the fear may be preserved as a **reported experience/rationale**, but it still should not be generalized into a hidden disposition.

## 2.6 Evidence preservation

Every coded episode should retain an evidence record containing:

| FieldRequired content     |                                                                              |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Episode ID                | Stable transcript-specific identifier                                        |
| Transcript locator        | Page, line, timestamp, or segment                                            |
| Evidence excerpt          | Short verbatim passage containing the behavioral evidence                    |
| Life period               | As precisely as reported                                                     |
| Situation                 | Concrete setting/event                                                       |
| Relevant people/roles     | Only as required to understand the episode                                   |
| Narrator perception       | What the narrator reports noticing or understanding                          |
| Choice/action             | What the narrator reports deciding or doing                                  |
| Non-action gate           | Awareness, opportunity, feasibility when non-action is coded                 |
| Sequence                  | Before/during/after relationships where relevant                             |
| Outcome                   | Reported immediate consequence, if known                                     |
| Narrator-stated rationale | Recorded verbatim or closely paraphrased; never upgraded into inferred cause |
| Memory certainty          | Definite / approximate / explicitly uncertain / conflicting recollection     |
| Exceptions                | Any reported counterepisode or qualification                                 |
| Context modifiers         | Relevant modifiers from Section 2.8                                          |
| Assigned codes            | Code IDs and substantive values                                              |

Third-party descriptions such as “my mother always said I was stubborn” are not primary evidence of the narrator's behavior unless the narrator supplies a concrete first-person episode or independently endorses specific actions.

## 2.7 Standard evidence states for every code

Every code below uses the following evidence-state field **in addition to its substantive value or values**.

**Observed (O):** A substantive value meets the code's recurrence requirement within the stated scope. “Observed” means recurrently reported behavior in the available narrative, not a permanent trait.

**Contradicted (C):** A specifically nominated value—such as a narrator's own generalization or an earlier within-code coding proposition—is directly opposed by qualifying concrete episodes, with the alternative behavior supported more strongly. The competing value must be recorded. External theories or hypotheses must never supply the proposition being tested during this first pass.

**Mixed (M):** Qualifying episodes within the same stated scope support materially different substantive values. Preserve each value and its episodes. If differences are clearly context-linked, produce separate context-bounded summaries as well.

**Insufficient evidence (IE):** Too few qualifying episodes, missing sequence information, unclear opportunity or feasibility, or other evidence gaps prevent assignment of a recurring value.

**Not applicable (NA):** The prerequisite situation for the code is affirmatively absent or structurally inapplicable within the episode or explicitly defined coding scope. Mere non-mention is not NA; it is usually IE.

## 2.8 Common context modifiers

These modifiers may accompany any code when supported by the narrative:

- life period;
- setting;
- task or activity type;
- relationship or role involved;
- whether action was voluntary, requested, assigned, or required;
- familiarity with the situation;
- reported stakes or expected consequences;
- time pressure;
- competing obligations;
- available time, money, information, equipment, or assistance;
- presence of an audience;
- formal authority or dependency;
- explicit rules or deadlines;
- availability of alternatives;
- previous relevant success or failure;
- reported physical or emotional state;
- whether the narrator expected the situation;
- whether the outcome was immediately knowable;
- and any narrator-identified circumstance that materially distinguished one episode from another.

Modifiers describe circumstances. They are not assumed to cause the behavior.

# 3. Coding procedure

For each transcript, coders first segment concrete episodes without attempting to characterize the narrator globally. They then apply any codes whose operational prerequisites are satisfied. Multiple codes may be applied to one episode when each is independently supported.

Substantive values may be recorded sequentially when behavior changes within an episode. For example, a narrator might first seek information, then compare alternatives, then choose, encounter a failure, change strategy, and continue pursuing the same goal. Those behaviors should not be compressed into a single label.

Only after episode coding should coders construct recurrence summaries.

No person is required to receive a value on every code.

# DOMAIN A — ENTERING AND ORGANIZING ACTION

## NBM-A01 — Action Initiation

**Short name:** Action initiation

**Operational definition:** The reported timing or manner by which the narrator moves from awareness of a feasible action opportunity or requirement to the first behavior directed toward it.

**Inclusion criteria:** There must be a recognizable action opportunity or demand, narrator awareness of it, reasonable opportunity to act, and sufficient timing information to characterize initiation.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude delays caused solely by an unavailable resource, blocked access, lack of awareness, or another circumstance making action infeasible. Exclude global statements such as “I start things quickly” without an episode. Preparation that is itself necessary task activity is not automatically a delay.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **A01-1 First-practical-opportunity start:** begins when a reasonable first opportunity is available.
- **A01-2 Self-selected delayed start:** knowingly postpones beginning to a later point.
- **A01-3 Externally prompted start:** begins after reminder, deadline signal, request, or other external prompt.
- **A01-4 Explicit non-initiation:** knowingly decides not to begin despite awareness, opportunity, and feasibility.
- **A01-5 Initiation timing unclear:** action occurred but available evidence cannot distinguish the above.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Episode coding requires the action opportunity, narrator awareness, and initiation timing or explicit non-initiation. Recurring-pattern status normally requires two independent qualifying episodes with the same value.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable episodes in which the narrator starts under materially different timing conditions, especially when the same type of task and similar constraints are reported.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Deadline proximity, task familiarity, competing demands, dependence on another person, expected consequences, time available, prior commitment.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I received the invoice at breakfast and paid it before going to work.” → A01-1.
2. “I knew the form was ready Monday, but decided I would deal with it Friday and did so then.” → A01-2.
3. “I wanted to submit it Monday, but the website was offline until Thursday.” → Do not code delayed initiation; feasibility was absent.

**Common coding mistakes:** Calling all late actions “delayed initiation”; counting required preparation as delay; inferring non-initiation because an action is not mentioned; treating one memorable episode as a recurrent pattern.

## NBM-A02 — Pre-Action Planning and Sequencing

**Short name:** Planning and sequencing

**Operational definition:** Explicitly reported arrangement of steps, timing, resources, or contingencies before or at the beginning of carrying out an intended action.

**Inclusion criteria:** The narrator must report forming or using an action sequence, schedule, checklist, preparation step, resource arrangement, or contingency before the relevant execution.

**Exclusion criteria:** Do not infer planning simply because an outcome was orderly or successful. Exclude retrospective explanations that do not indicate prior preparation. Absence of mentioned planning is IE unless the narrator explicitly says they acted without planning.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **A02-1 Direct action with explicitly little/no prior planning.**
- **A02-2 Single-step preparation.**
- **A02-3 Multi-step sequencing.**
- **A02-4 Time/deadline scheduling.**
- **A02-5 Resource preparation.**
- **A02-6 Contingency preparation:** explicit “if X, then Y” planning.
- Multiple subcodes may coexist.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** A reported action plus explicit evidence about whether and how preparation occurred. Recurrence normally requires two independent qualifying episodes.

**Counterevidence:** Similar episodes in which the narrator explicitly proceeds with materially less or more advance preparation.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Complexity, familiarity, stakes, deadline, coordination needs, available preparation time.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “The night before, I listed the three offices I had to visit and put the documents in separate folders.” → A02-3 and A02-5.
2. “I just walked into the meeting and decided what to say once they asked.” → A02-1.
3. “The project turned out very organized.” → No planning code unless prior preparation is reported.

**Common coding mistakes:** Inferring planning from competence; treating all thinking as planning; coding an imposed schedule as the narrator's own planning unless the narrator actively arranged or adopted it.

---

## NBM-A03 — Choice Resolution

**Short name:** Choice resolution

**Operational definition:** The behavior by which the narrator selects, postpones, tests, or leaves unresolved a choice among two or more recognized viable alternatives.

**Inclusion criteria:** At least two options must be recognized by the narrator, and the episode must report how selection or non-selection occurred.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude forced situations with no meaningful alternative, routine actions where no choice is reported, and decisions reconstructed solely by the coder.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **A03-1 Selects without reported comparison.**
- **A03-2 Explicit criteria comparison.**
- **A03-3 Consults another person before selecting.**
- **A03-4 Tests or samples an option before final selection.**
- **A03-5 Defers with an explicit revisit point or condition.**
- **A03-6 Remains unresolved despite established opportunity to decide.**
- Sequential subcodes may be retained.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Two recognized alternatives plus a reported selection process or established non-selection. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable decisions resolved using another process.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Number of options, reversibility, time pressure, stakes, familiarity, availability of advice or trial periods.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I compared rent, commute, and lease length for both apartments and chose the second.” → A03-2.
2. “I tried the software for a week before choosing whether to subscribe.” → A03-4.
3. “There was only one bus left, so I took it.” → Not a qualifying choice episode.

**Common coding mistakes:** Treating any action as a decision episode; confusing information gathering with choice resolution; inferring indecision when the narrator simply lacked an opportunity to decide.

# DOMAIN B — CONTINUING, CHANGING, AND REVIEWING ACTION

## NBM-B01 — Goal Continuation After Friction

**Short name:** Continuation after friction

**Operational definition:** What the narrator does with an ongoing goal after encountering a reported obstacle, rejection, failure, increased cost, interruption, or other difficulty that makes continuation harder.

**Inclusion criteria:** There must be an identifiable ongoing goal, an explicit friction event, and post-friction behavior.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude tasks that merely end normally, situations with no obstacle, and episodes where continuation becomes impossible rather than optional.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **B01-1 Continues toward the same goal without interruption.**
- **B01-2 Increases or adds effort toward the same goal.**
- **B01-3 Pauses and later resumes the same goal.**
- **B01-4 Continues the goal through a different route.**
- **B01-5 Reduces or narrows the original goal.**
- **B01-6 Stops pursuing the goal.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Goal + friction + reported subsequent behavior. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable setbacks followed by a different continuation/disengagement response.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Number of prior setbacks, cost of continuation, goal importance as stated by narrator, alternatives available, external barriers, time remaining.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “The first application was rejected, so I submitted to four other programs.” → B01-2.
2. “After the injury I stopped training for two months, then returned to the same race plan.” → B01-3.
3. “The building closed permanently, so I could not finish the course there.” → Do not code voluntary stopping unless another feasible continuation route is established.

**Common coding mistakes:** Calling persistence a personality characteristic; treating forced termination as voluntary disengagement; collapsing persistence and strategy change into one code.

---

## NBM-B02 — Strategy Modification After Feedback

**Short name:** Strategy modification

**Operational definition:** A reported change, or deliberate lack of change, in the method used to pursue an objective after the narrator receives information about how the current method is working.

**Inclusion criteria:** A method must be identifiable, some result or feedback must be observed, and the narrator's subsequent method must be reported.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude method changes caused by unrelated circumstances unless the narrator links them to task feedback. Do not infer learning merely because later behavior differs.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **B02-1 Maintains the same method after feedback.**
- **B02-2 Makes a limited adjustment.**
- **B02-3 Replaces the method substantially.**
- **B02-4 Seeks additional input and then modifies the method.**
- **B02-5 Stops using the method without a replacement reported.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Initial method, identifiable feedback/outcome, subsequent method, and a sequence indicating that the feedback preceded the response. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar feedback followed by a different level of modification.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Feedback clarity, source of feedback, number of prior attempts, cost of switching, alternative methods available.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “Customers kept missing the instructions, so I rewrote them as three numbered steps.” → B02-2.
2. “When the second advertising campaign failed, we abandoned social media ads and started doing local demonstrations.” → B02-3.
3. “I changed my schedule because my train time changed.” → Not B02 unless task-performance feedback also drove the change.

**Common coding mistakes:** Assuming every change reflects feedback; conflating goal abandonment with method change; coding a different behavior months later without an explicit feedback sequence.

---

## NBM-B03 — Commitment Follow-Through

**Short name:** Commitment follow-through

**Operational definition:** The relationship between an explicitly reported prior commitment, promise, agreement, or definite intention and the narrator's later action.

**Inclusion criteria:** The earlier commitment must be identifiable and specific enough to establish what later behavior would constitute completion, modification, or noncompletion.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude wishes, preferences, hopes, and vague ambitions unless the narrator describes them as an actual commitment or intended action.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **B03-1 Completed substantially as committed.**
- **B03-2 Completed after delay.**
- **B03-3 Renegotiated or modified before completion was due.**
- **B03-4 Partially completed.**
- **B03-5 Explicitly rescinded or withdrew the commitment.**
- **B03-6 Did not complete despite established opportunity and feasibility.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** A specific prior commitment plus subsequent outcome. Noncompletion requires the Awareness–Opportunity–Feasibility gate. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Other comparable commitments with different follow-through outcomes.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Commitment to self versus others, voluntary versus imposed commitment, duration, difficulty, changing circumstances, renegotiation opportunities.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I told her I would send the file by noon, and I emailed it at eleven.” → B03-1.
2. “I had agreed to volunteer Saturday, but on Thursday I called them and changed to the following week.” → B03-3.
3. “I always wanted to learn Arabic but never did.” → Not B03 unless a concrete commitment is established.

**Common coding mistakes:** Equating aspirations with commitments; coding unavoidable noncompletion as failed follow-through; ignoring explicit renegotiation and treating it as simple noncompletion.

## NBM-B04 — Routine Enactment

**Short name:** Routine enactment

**Operational definition:** Repetition, interruption, alteration, or discontinuation of substantially similar behavior in a recurring situation or cue context.

**Inclusion criteria:** Evidence must establish repeated occasions rather than a single episode.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude one-off sequences, purely hypothetical routines, and unsupported statements such as “I always exercise” without concrete recurrence evidence.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **B04-1 Stable repeated enactment.**
- **B04-2 Repetition with planned variation.**
- **B04-3 Intermittent enactment.**
- **B04-4 Deliberately changed an established routine.**
- **B04-5 Discontinued an established routine.**
- **B04-6 Newly established recurring routine.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Normally three identifiable occurrences, or two concrete occurrences plus an explicit frequency statement covering additional occasions. A bare frequency claim without concrete examples remains IE.

**Counterevidence:** Qualifying occasions when the expected routine was not enacted or was replaced.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Cue or schedule, external requirements, frequency, location, workdays versus non-workdays, availability of resources.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “Every Monday for those six weeks I called my father after dinner; I remember doing it after the first, third, and final classes.” → B04-1.
2. “I used to shop every Saturday, but after moving I switched to Wednesday evenings.” → B04-4.
3. “I usually read before bed.” with no concrete instance → Insufficient for recurrent coding.

**Common coding mistakes:** Taking words such as “always” literally without episode support; calling externally scheduled attendance a self-created habit; ignoring documented exceptions.

---

## NBM-B05 — Outcome Checking and Review

**Short name:** Outcome checking

**Operational definition:** Deliberate behavior after or near completion of an action to verify accuracy, completion, reception, consequences, or correspondence with an intended result.

**Inclusion criteria:** The narrator must report a distinct checking, verification, review, or confirmation behavior.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude outcomes that are simply obvious without checking. Do not infer checking from successful performance.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **B05-1 Direct self-check.**
- **B05-2 External confirmation from another person or system.**
- **B05-3 Repeated checking of the same outcome.**
- **B05-4 Retrospective review after the outcome is known.**
- **B05-5 Explicitly does not check despite established awareness, opportunity, and feasibility.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** A completed or near-completed action plus explicit review behavior, or a qualifying non-check under the non-action gate. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar tasks with materially different checking behavior.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Stakes, reversibility, error cost, external verification requirements, deadline pressure, outcome visibility.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “After entering the figures, I recalculated the totals once before sending them.” → B05-1.
2. “I checked the tracking page four times that afternoon.” → B05-3.
3. “The alarm rang, so I knew the test had finished.” → Merely perceiving an automatic signal is not necessarily deliberate checking.

**Common coding mistakes:** Inferring carefulness; coding passive receipt of feedback as active checking; interpreting repeated checking as pathological or assigning a motive not stated.

# DOMAIN C — OBTAINING INFORMATION AND ASSISTANCE

## NBM-C01 — Information Seeking

**Short name:** Information seeking

**Operational definition:** Behavior undertaken to obtain task-relevant facts, clarification, comparison information, or direct observations when the narrator recognizes an information gap.

**Inclusion criteria:** An unknown or uncertainty must be recognizable, and the narrator must report what they did or explicitly chose not to do about obtaining information.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude unsolicited information received passively. Exclude social contact whose purpose is assistance rather than acquiring information unless information is also explicitly sought.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **C01-1 Asks a person for information.**
- **C01-2 Consults a written/digital/reference source.**
- **C01-3 Compares multiple information sources.**
- **C01-4 Observes or tests directly to learn.**
- **C01-5 Stops searching and acts using available information.**
- **C01-6 Proceeds without seeking additional information despite an explicitly recognized gap and feasible opportunity.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Recognized information gap plus relevant action/non-action. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar uncertainty episodes handled by another information-seeking pattern.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Information availability, stakes, source accessibility, time pressure, narrator's prior familiarity.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I didn't know which permit I needed, so I called the city office.” → C01-1.
2. “I read reviews from five sites and compared the specifications.” → C01-2 and C01-3.
3. “My coworker happened to tell me the rule during lunch.” → Not information seeking unless the narrator had actively solicited it.

**Common coding mistakes:** Inferring curiosity; treating all conversation as information seeking; equating extensive search with indecision; coding missing search behavior without the non-action gate.

## NBM-C02 — Help Seeking and Help Use

**Short name:** Help seeking/use

**Operational definition:** Behavior involving requesting, accepting, declining, or using another person's assistance in addressing a concrete difficulty or task.

**Inclusion criteria:** A concrete need, difficulty, or task must be present, and another person must be available or approached as a source of assistance.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude ordinary interdependent teamwork when no one is specifically assisting another; code that under NBM-C03. Exclude assumptions that someone “should have” asked for help.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **C02-1 Requests help before attempting alone.**
- **C02-2 Requests help after a solo attempt.**
- **C02-3 Accepts offered help.**
- **C02-4 Declines offered help.**
- **C02-5 Delegates a defined portion of the task.**
- **C02-6 Requests practical assistance.**
- **C02-7 Requests emotional/supportive presence.**
- **C02-8 Does not request available help despite established awareness, opportunity, and feasibility.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Concrete difficulty/task plus explicit assistance behavior or qualifying non-action. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable situations where help is handled differently.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Relationship, expertise of helper, availability, privacy, stakes, prior attempts, prior responses from helpers.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “After I couldn't get the program to run, I asked Mei to look at the error.” → C02-2.
2. “My neighbor offered to carry the boxes and I said yes.” → C02-3.
3. “Nobody helped me with the move.” → Not evidence that the narrator declined or failed to seek help unless opportunity is established.

**Common coding mistakes:** Inferring dependence or independence; treating informational questions automatically as help-seeking without coding the informational component separately; assuming non-request equals refusal.

---

## NBM-C03 — Coordination With Others

**Short name:** Coordination

**Operational definition:** Behavior intended to align roles, timing, responsibilities, or actions among people whose activities are interdependent in a concrete task.

**Inclusion criteria:** At least two people's actions must depend on one another in the reported situation.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude purely solo tasks, ordinary conversation, and one-directional requests for assistance where joint alignment is unnecessary.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **C03-1 Initiates coordination.**
- **C03-2 Responds to another person's coordination attempt.**
- **C03-3 Explicitly divides roles or tasks.**
- **C03-4 Synchronizes timing or sequence.**
- **C03-5 Renegotiates roles/timing after circumstances change.**
- **C03-6 Proceeds independently despite established interdependence and feasible opportunity to coordinate.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Interdependence plus a reported alignment action or qualifying non-coordination. Two episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar joint tasks handled through different degrees or forms of coordination.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Group size, authority, deadline, role clarity, prior agreements, communication access.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “Before starting, I asked who would contact clients and who would prepare the figures.” → C03-1 and C03-3.
2. “When her train was delayed, we moved my part of the installation to the morning.” → C03-5.
3. “I asked him how to fix the printer.” → Usually C01/C02, not C03, unless a joint task also required alignment.

**Common coding mistakes:** Treating all collaboration as coordination without identifying an alignment behavior; treating help-seeking and coordination as interchangeable.

# DOMAIN D — INTERPERSONAL REQUESTS, DISAGREEMENT, AND REPAIR

## NBM-D01 — Response to Requests and Personal Limits

**Short name:** Request/limit response

**Operational definition:** The narrator's reported response when another person makes a request or demand that requires a decision about accepting, refusing, modifying, delaying, or ending the requested commitment.

**Inclusion criteria:** A concrete request/demand and the narrator's response must both be reported.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude voluntary helping initiated by the narrator when no request occurs. Do not infer an internal “boundary” if the narrator reports no relevant limit or conflict.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **D01-1 Accepts request substantially as made.**
- **D01-2 Refuses request.**
- **D01-3 Accepts with conditions or negotiates scope.**
- **D01-4 Delays giving an answer.**
- **D01-5 Initially accepts, then renegotiates or withdraws.**
- **D01-6 Complies after explicitly expressing inability or unwillingness.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Explicit request/demand plus explicit response. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable requests receiving different responses.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Relationship, authority, cost to narrator, urgency, competing commitments, prior refusals/agreements.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “He asked me to cover Sunday. I said I could do the morning but not the afternoon.” → D01-3.
2. “She asked me for the loan and I told her no.” → D01-2.
3. “I cooked dinner because I knew they were busy.” → Not D01 unless a request or demand occurred.

**Common coding mistakes:** Labeling acceptance as “people pleasing”; labeling refusal as “assertive”; inferring a personal limit the narrator never identified; failing to distinguish conditional acceptance from full acceptance.

## NBM-D02 — Disagreement Handling

**Short name:** Disagreement handling

**Operational definition:** Observable/reported behavior after the narrator recognizes a disagreement, dispute, or interpersonal conflict with another person.

**Inclusion criteria:** The disagreement must be explicit in the narrative, and a subsequent response must be reported.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude private dislike with no recognized disagreement, conflict inferred only by the coder, and ordinary differences in preference that produce no interpersonal response.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **D02-1 Asks questions or seeks clarification.**
- **D02-2 States own position directly.**
- **D02-3 Accepts or accommodates the other person's position.**
- **D02-4 Proposes compromise or alternative arrangement.**
- **D02-5 Postpones the discussion.**
- **D02-6 Leaves or ends the interaction.**
- **D02-7 Involves a third party or mediator.**
- **D02-8 Increases the interaction's confrontational intensity**, such as louder speech, insults, threats, or expanded stakes, only when explicitly reported.

Sequential values may all be retained.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Recognized disagreement plus subsequent behavior. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar disagreements handled differently.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Relationship, authority, audience, stakes, duration of relationship, time pressure, perceived consequences.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I told him I disagreed and explained which part of the proposal I wanted changed.” → D02-2.
2. “We were getting nowhere, so I said we should discuss it tomorrow.” → D02-5.
3. “I disliked her decision but never discussed it with her.” → No interpersonal disagreement-response code unless an explicit interaction followed.

**Common coding mistakes:** Calling withdrawal “avoidant,” direct speech “assertive,” or raised intensity “aggressive personality”; treating a single disagreement style as global; ignoring changes within the same interaction.

---

## NBM-D03 — Interpersonal Repair

**Short name:** Interpersonal repair

**Operational definition:** Behavior taken after the narrator recognizes or believes that their own action, communication, or omission produced harm, offense, misunderstanding, or relational disruption.

**Inclusion criteria:** Narrator awareness of an interpersonal impact plus subsequent response must be reported.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude generic politeness, conflict occurring before any acknowledged impact, and repairs carried out solely by the other person.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **D03-1 Acknowledges own action or impact.**
- **D03-2 Apologizes.**
- **D03-3 Clarifies facts or intended meaning.**
- **D03-4 Offers correction, restitution, or replacement action.**
- **D03-5 Asks the affected person what response is needed.**
- **D03-6 Delays repair but later acts.**
- **D03-7 Ends/avoids contact after recognizing the impact.**
- **D03-8 Takes no repair action despite awareness, opportunity, and feasibility.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Recognized interpersonal impact plus response/non-response. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable interpersonal disruptions handled differently.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Severity as reported, relationship, whether responsibility is disputed, elapsed time, availability of restitution.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I realized the message sounded accusatory, so I called her and explained what I had meant.” → D03-3.
2. “I broke his tool and bought a replacement that afternoon.” → D03-4.
3. “He was upset with me, but I didn't know that until six months later.” → Earlier non-repair cannot be coded because awareness was absent.

**Common coding mistakes:** Assuming apology implies admission of fault beyond what was stated; coding lack of immediate repair when the narrator did not yet know of the harm; treating reconciliation outcome as equivalent to repair behavior.

---

# DOMAIN E — UNCERTAINTY, TRADEOFFS, AND FORMAL CONSTRAINTS

## NBM-E01 — Engagement With Unfamiliar Options

**Short name:** Unfamiliar-option engagement

**Operational definition:** What the narrator does when encountering an option, activity, environment, or course of action that they explicitly describe as new, unfamiliar, or having substantially uncertain consequences.

**Inclusion criteria:** Unfamiliarity or uncertainty must be reported by the narrator rather than inferred by the coder.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude ordinary choices among familiar options and situations that coders merely regard as objectively risky.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **E01-1 Engages directly.**
- **E01-2 Uses a limited trial or reversible first step.**
- **E01-3 Observes before participating.**
- **E01-4 Seeks information before engaging.**
- **E01-5 Defers engagement.**
- **E01-6 Declines the unfamiliar option.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Explicit unfamiliarity/uncertainty plus reported response. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Other unfamiliar situations handled differently.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Reversibility, stakes, presence of experienced others, time to decide, prior analogous experience.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I had never kayaked, so I first took the short beginner session before deciding about the full trip.” → E01-2.
2. “I didn't understand the software, so I watched two demonstrations before trying it.” → E01-3 and possibly C01.
3. “Investing is risky, so the coder marks her as cautious.” → Invalid unless the narrator actually describes the option as uncertain and reports a response.

**Common coding mistakes:** Converting the code into “risk tolerance” or “openness”; inferring unfamiliarity from the coder's knowledge; failing to dual-code information seeking when independently present.

---

## NBM-E02 — Resource Allocation Under Constraint

**Short name:** Resource allocation

**Operational definition:** How the narrator allocates a clearly limited resource among two or more competing demands.

**Inclusion criteria:** A resource constraint and genuine tradeoff must be explicit. Resources may include time, money, attention, physical effort, access, space, or another concretely limited means.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude ordinary choices with no stated constraint and coder assumptions that a resource “must have been” scarce.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **E02-1 Concentrates the resource on one demand.**
- **E02-2 Divides the resource across demands.**
- **E02-3 Sequences demands over time.**
- **E02-4 Preserves a reserve or buffer.**
- **E02-5 Reallocates after circumstances change.**
- **E02-6 Drops or postpones a competing demand because of the constraint.**

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Explicitly limited resource, at least two competing uses, and reported allocation. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Similar constraint situations managed differently.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Resource type, degree of scarcity, deadlines, obligations, consequences, availability of replacement resources.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I only had $300 left that month, so I paid the electricity bill and postponed buying the chair.” → E02-1/E02-6.
2. “I had two free hours, so I spent one on the report and one preparing dinner.” → E02-2.
3. “I chose the blue chair instead of the red one.” → Not resource allocation unless a meaningful resource constraint is established.

**Common coding mistakes:** Converting spending choices into “frugality”; assuming scarcity; judging one allocation as better or more responsible.

---

## NBM-E03 — Response to Explicit Rules or Procedures

**Short name:** Rule/procedure response

**Operational definition:** Behavior when an explicit rule, instruction, agreed procedure, or formal requirement applies and the narrator has an opportunity to follow it, clarify it, seek modification, or depart from it.

**Inclusion criteria:** The rule or procedure must be identifiable rather than inferred from a vague social norm.

**Exclusion criteria:** Exclude assumed cultural expectations, unstated conventions, and behavior where compliance was impossible.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **E03-1 Follows the stated rule/procedure.**
- **E03-2 Seeks clarification.**
- **E03-3 Requests an exception or modification.**
- **E03-4 Uses an alternative explicitly permitted within the rule.**
- **E03-5 Openly departs from the rule.**
- **E03-6 Conceals departure from the rule**, only when concealment is explicitly reported.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Explicit rule/procedure, narrator awareness, and relevant response. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable rule-governed situations with another response.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Rule clarity, enforcement, authority, stated purpose, consequences, available exception process.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “The policy required approval, so I submitted the request before buying it.” → E03-1.
2. “The procedure didn't cover contractors, so I asked HR which rule applied.” → E03-2.
3. “Everyone normally dressed formally.” → Not E03 unless an explicit rule or agreed requirement is established.

**Common coding mistakes:** Treating conventional behavior as rule compliance; judging compliance or departure morally; inferring covert behavior without direct report.

---

# DOMAIN F — ACTION DURING STRONGLY REPORTED SUBJECTIVE STATES

## NBM-F01 — Action During Strong Reported Emotion or Bodily State

**Short name:** Strong-state action

**Operational definition:** What the narrator reports doing while explicitly experiencing a strong emotional or bodily state that is salient to the episode.

**Inclusion criteria:** The narrator must explicitly report the state and the behavior occurring during or immediately in response to it. Examples may include anger, fear, excitement, sadness, embarrassment, pain, exhaustion, or other narrator-described states.

**Exclusion criteria:** Coders may not infer the state from facial expression, circumstances, or what “most people would feel.” Diagnoses and trait labels are excluded. Mild or background states need not be coded unless they clearly organize the episode.

**Possible values/subcodes:**

- **F01-1 Continues the ongoing task/activity.**
- **F01-2 Delays or pauses action.**
- **F01-3 Leaves or changes the immediate environment.**
- **F01-4 Verbally communicates the state or an immediate need.**
- **F01-5 Contacts another person for support.**
- **F01-6 Takes a deliberate physical or attentional action before proceeding**, such as sitting down, walking, drinking water, counting, or another specifically reported act.
- **F01-7 Immediately carries out an action the narrator explicitly links to the state.**
- Multiple sequential values may be recorded.

**Evidence states:** O / C / M / IE / NA.

**Minimum required evidence:** Explicit narrator-reported strong state plus temporally linked behavior. Two independent episodes for recurrence.

**Counterevidence:** Comparable reported strong-state episodes followed by different actions.

**Relevant context modifiers:** Narrator's own description of state/intensity, trigger, audience, physical setting, available exit/support, task demands, consequences.

**Fictional boundary examples:**

1. “I was furious, so I told him I needed ten minutes and walked outside.” → F01-4 and F01-3.
2. “I was terrified during the presentation, but I kept reading the slides until I finished.” → F01-1.
3. “Her partner left her, so she must have been devastated.” → Invalid; the state cannot be inferred.

**Common coding mistakes:** Assigning psychiatric or personality meaning to the state; assuming the behavior successfully “regulated” emotion; inferring emotion from circumstances; overlooking explicit continued action because the narrative emphasizes feeling.

# 4. Cross-code decision rules

## 4.1 Multiple codes are allowed

One episode can legitimately receive several codes when separate operational criteria are met.

Example:

> “I had never prepared a grant before, so I read two examples, asked a colleague what reviewers expected, made a three-stage schedule, submitted the draft, received criticism, revised my approach, and resubmitted.”

Potential coding includes E01 unfamiliar-option engagement, C01 information seeking, C02 help seeking, A02 planning, B02 strategy modification, and B01 goal continuation.

This is not double-counting if each code represents a separately documented behavior.

## 4.2 Do not collapse sequences

If the narrator changes behavior over time, preserve the sequence.

“First I refused, then after she explained the emergency I agreed to cover one hour” should not be reduced simply to “accepted request.” Record the initial response and subsequent modification.

## 4.3 Code actions, not presumed functions

The same behavior may serve different purposes, and coders should not assign its purpose unless stated.

Leaving a room during a disagreement may be:

- D02-6 because the interaction ended;
- F01-3 if a strong reported state was also present;
- but it must not automatically be labeled avoidance, self-regulation, hostility, or conflict aversion.

## 4.4 Distinguish goals from methods

NBM-B01 asks whether pursuit of the **goal** continued.

NBM-B02 asks whether the **method** changed.

A narrator can therefore change strategy while continuing the same goal.

## 4.5 Distinguish information from assistance

NBM-C01 concerns obtaining information.

NBM-C02 concerns obtaining or responding to assistance.

An expert consultation can legitimately receive both codes when the narrator both seeks facts and receives practical help.

## 4.6 Distinguish planning from delay

Planning is task-directed preparation. Delay is temporal postponement of initiating the relevant action.

Do not code A01-2 merely because someone spent substantial time performing necessary preparation.

## 4.7 Distinguish insufficient evidence from not applicable

Use **IE** when the interview does not tell the coder enough.

Use **NA** only when the prerequisite circumstance is affirmatively absent.

Example: If an episode contains no reported disagreement, D02 may be NA for that episode. If a life-history section never discusses whether disagreements occurred, a person-level D02 summary is IE, not NA.

---

# 5. Person-level recurrence summary format

Person-level summaries should remain behaviorally phrased and context-bounded.

Recommended format:

**Code:** NBM-B01\
**Scope:** Employment-related setbacks, ages approximately 28–34\
**Substantive value:** B01-3 pauses and later resumes same goal\
**Evidence state:** Observed\
**Supporting episodes:** E17, E29, E41\
**Counterepisodes:** E33 — stopped goal after cost increased substantially\
**Context qualification:** Supporting episodes involved setbacks the narrator described as reversible; E33 involved loss of required funding.\
**Source certainty:** Two definite recollections; one approximate-date recollection.\
**Permitted summary:** “Across three reported employment setbacks described as reversible, the narrator paused and later resumed pursuit of the same goal. One differently constrained episode ended in discontinuation.”

Not permitted:

> “Persistent person.”

> “High resilience.”

> “Strong achievement orientation.”

> “Type X.”

---

# 6. Handling narrator generalizations

Global narrator statements may be stored in a separate **Narrator Claim** field but are not themselves substantive behavioral evidence.

Example:

> “I never ask anyone for help.”

If the transcript contains two concrete episodes in which the narrator requested assistance, the claim may be marked **contradicted by episode evidence** under NBM-C02.

If the transcript contains one episode of not requesting help and no other concrete cases, the recurring pattern remains **insufficient evidence** even though the narrator used the word “never.”

Coder agreement should depend on episode evidence, not rhetorical strength.

---

# 7. Missingness and uncertainty

Missing information must remain visible rather than resolved by inference.

Use explicit annotations for:

**Unknown:** Relevant fact was not provided.

**Approximate:** Narrator gives uncertain timing, number, sequence, or detail.

**Conflicting recall:** Narrator provides internally inconsistent recollections.

**Unclear agency:** It is uncertain whether the narrator chose the action or was compelled.

**Unclear feasibility:** It cannot be established whether an alternative action was realistically available.

**Unclear sequence:** The order needed for a code cannot be established.

A low-detail episode may still support some codes while remaining unusable for others.

For example, “I eventually changed jobs” may document an action but cannot establish A01 initiation timing, A02 planning, A03 choice process, or B01 response to friction without additional episode information.

---

# 8. Counterevidence and exceptions

Counterevidence must never be discarded merely because a majority pattern has been identified.

For every recurrent code summary, coders should search for:

1. episodes with the same general prerequisite situation but another substantive value;
2. explicit exceptions supplied by the narrator;
3. episodes occurring in different contexts that may delimit the scope of the pattern;
4. later episodes showing behavioral change over time.

An apparent contradiction should first be checked for differences in reported context.

If the context difference is clear and repeatedly associated with different behavior, produce context-specific summaries.

If no reliable contextual distinction explains the variation, retain **mixed**.

No numerical “overall personality score” should be generated from these codes in the first-pass coding stage.

---

# 9. Behavioral change over time

Change should be represented directly rather than interpreted as growth, decline, maturation, deterioration, healing, or any other normative process.

Permitted:

> “Earlier episodes show D01-1 acceptance of requests; three later episodes show D01-3 conditional acceptance.”

Not permitted:

> “The narrator became healthier at setting boundaries.”

When a behavioral pattern changes across life periods, each period may receive its own context-bounded summary if enough episodes exist.

---

# 11. Likely overlaps requiring explicit coder attention

Several code pairs are expected to co-occur. Co-occurrence is not an error, but pilot training should ensure coders can tell why each code applies.

**A01 initiation vs. A02 planning:** Preparation may itself be the beginning of task action. Coders need agreement on what counts as the relevant action start.

**A02 planning vs. C01 information seeking:** Research may be one part of preparation, but information seeking requires an identifiable information gap.

**A03 choice resolution vs. C01 information seeking:** Information acquisition is not the same event as selecting among alternatives.

**B01 goal continuation vs. B02 strategy modification:** The first concerns whether the goal continues; the second concerns whether the method changes.

**B01 continuation vs. B03 commitment follow-through:** Continuing a goal after difficulty does not necessarily involve a prior explicit commitment.

**B03 commitment follow-through vs. B04 routine enactment:** Repeated fulfillment of commitments can look routine-like, but routine requires repeated cue-context enactment.

**B02 strategy modification vs. B05 outcome checking:** Checking can occur without changing strategy, and strategy change can occur after feedback received without a separate checking act.

**C01 information seeking vs. C02 help seeking:** Asking an expert may satisfy both if the person supplies both information and assistance.

**C02 help seeking vs. C03 coordination:** Help is asymmetric with respect to the immediate difficulty; coordination concerns interdependent actions.

**D01 request response vs. D02 disagreement handling:** Refusing a request can produce a disagreement. Code both only if a disagreement actually occurs.

**D02 disagreement vs. D03 repair:** Repair follows perceived interpersonal impact; disagreement by itself does not establish a repair episode.

**E01 unfamiliar-option engagement vs. A03 choice resolution:** New situations may also involve competing options, but unfamiliarity must be explicitly reported for E01.

**E01 unfamiliar-option engagement vs. C01 information seeking:** Seeking information before a novel activity may receive both codes.

**E02 resource allocation vs. A03 choice resolution:** Resource allocation requires explicit scarcity and competing uses; ordinary choice does not.

**F01 strong-state action vs. D02 disagreement or B01 persistence:** F01 adds information only when the narrator explicitly reports a strong subjective state occurring with the behavior.

# 12. Areas requiring pilot reliability testing

The first reliability pilot should specifically test the following judgment points rather than relying only on overall agreement.

### Episode segmentation

Coders may differ on whether several reported moments form one episode or multiple independent behavioral opportunities. Training examples should include extended stories containing multiple decisions.

### Independence of recurrence evidence

Coders should be tested on whether two references are genuinely independent episodes versus repeated discussion of one event.

### Awareness–Opportunity–Feasibility gate

This is likely to be one of the most important sources of disagreement. Pilot cases should contain ambiguous omissions, partial opportunities, social constraints, unavailable resources, and time-limited feasibility.

### IE versus NA

Coders commonly substitute “not mentioned” for “not applicable.” Reliability should be measured specifically for this distinction.

### Planning versus action initiation

Coders need shared rules for deciding when preparation constitutes the beginning of the task rather than postponement.

### Choice resolution versus information seeking

Cases involving extensive research followed by a decision should test whether coders consistently separate the two processes.

### Goal continuation versus strategy modification

Pilot transcripts should include continuation with unchanged methods, continuation with changed methods, narrowed goals, and outright goal termination.

### Commitment versus aspiration

Reliability testing should examine phrases such as “I wanted to,” “I meant to,” “I planned to,” “I promised,” and “I had decided,” because coders may differ about whether a commitment was sufficiently definite.

### Routine frequency threshold

Coders should agree on what constitutes distinct occurrences and whether a frequency statement plus concrete examples meets B04's recurrence threshold.

### Help versus coordination

Joint work episodes should be tested for whether assistance is truly asymmetric or whether actions are mutually interdependent.

### Request negotiation versus disagreement

Conditional acceptance, bargaining, refusal, and later conflict should be presented in boundary cases.

### Repair prerequisites

Coders should be tested on whether narrator awareness of impact occurred before the alleged non-repair period.

### Unfamiliarity

E01 should be piloted to ensure coders do not substitute their own judgment about objective novelty or danger for the narrator's stated experience.

### Resource constraints

Reliability may be poor when scarcity is implied rather than stated. The pilot should test how much evidence is required to establish a genuine tradeoff.

### Rule versus convention

E03 should be tested with written rules, direct instructions, informal expectations, customs, and ambiguous “usual practice.”

### Strong reported states

Pilot testing should determine whether coders reliably identify when a reported emotion or bodily state is sufficiently salient to invoke F01 without converting ordinary affect into a special code.

### Sequential multi-value coding

Episodes where behavior changes—refusal followed by negotiation, pause followed by continuation, information seeking followed by trial—should be tested to ensure coders preserve sequence rather than select only the final act.

### Context splitting versus “mixed”

Coders may differ about whether different behavior reflects a context-bounded pattern or genuine within-context variability. Adjudication rules should be refined after examining actual disagreements.

### Counterevidence search

Reliability should include whether coders identify and retain contradictory episodes, rather than only whether they recognize supporting episodes.

---

# 13. Recommended pilot outputs

The pilot should evaluate reliability separately for:

- episode boundary identification;
- whether each code applies;
- substantive subcode assignment;
- evidence-state assignment;
- non-action eligibility;
- context modifiers;
- counterevidence identification;
- and person-level recurrence summaries.

Confusion matrices are especially important because a superficially acceptable aggregate agreement rate can conceal systematic confusion between adjacent values such as B01-3 versus B01-6, C02 versus C03, or IE versus NA.

Low-reliability distinctions should be revised, merged, or removed before substantive analysis. They should not be retained merely because they appear conceptually interesting.

# 14. First-pass replication constraint

This draft contains only behavioral distinctions developed from the requirements of episode-level autobiographical coding. No code should be retrospectively renamed, merged, subdivided, reweighted, scored, or reinterpreted to increase correspondence with a subsequently revealed external hypothesis, theory, model, typology, test, demographic classification, diagnostic scheme, biological theory, spiritual system, or other comparison framework.

Any later mapping to an external model should therefore be performed as a **separate analytic layer**, with the original episode codes, source excerpts, context modifiers, missingness, counterevidence, and versioned codebook preserved.

If later researchers propose derived variables, composites, scales, hypothesis-specific mappings, or model comparisons, those products should receive new identifiers and a new version rather than silently changing this first-pass behavioral record.

**This exact first-pass output should be preserved unchanged before any external hypothesis or model comparison is revealed.**
