# Human Design — Scenario-First Survey Design

## Refined scenario candidates · 19 September 2026

**Status: proposed wording for discussion, not an approved implementation specification.**

The aim is to make the next answer easier to give, not to make the participant identify their own personality pattern before the interview has begun. This batch contains six possible entry scenes, not six required questions or a claim of complete coverage. All example replies below are invented to illustrate interviewer behavior. None describes the owner or constitutes participant data.

The inference VM has not been contacted or woken during this pass. No runtime, deployment, model/provider setting, accepted evidence contract, or scoring rule has been changed.

## 1. What changed from the handoff

The invitation no longer says the person has **finally** obtained time to themselves. That word supplies a reason to protect the time before the person has answered. The revised scene establishes an ordinary preference without making either choice the virtuous one.

The partner scene retains the owner's interest in **recurring anxiety**, rather than quietly replacing it with a single brief anxious moment. Recurrence, the presence of a request for help, and physical proximity are different conditions. They should not all change in the same follow-up.

The invitation now names reading, the opportunity names a cooking class, and the cancellation specifies a mild cold. These make the starting situations less dependent on invented context, without turning the scenes into exhaustive contracts.

The set includes private activity, disrupted plans, uncertainty, and disagreement as well as interpersonal requests. The family names below organize this editorial batch; they are not latent traits or a closed scoring taxonomy.

There is also an important attribution rule: **a preference supplied by the scene is not a preference discovered about the person.** For example, a scene that says “you would prefer somewhere quieter” cannot establish that the respondent generally prefers quiet places. The answer concerns what they would do with that supplied preference.

## 2. Participant-facing entry

A possible short introduction, shown once:

> These are everyday situations, not tests of the right thing to do. Answer as yourself. You can say what changes your answer, use a real example, or tell me the situation does not fit.

Do not require people to select an evidence category before answering. The interviewer should preserve whether their words express an expectation, a usual tendency, a remembered event, or something else. When that is genuinely unclear and matters to the next step, ask one clarification rather than silently deciding.

The headings and notes below are for design review. Only one scene and its response task would be presented at a time. Free text stays available throughout.

## 3. Six candidate opening cards

### A. A casual invitation during personal time

> You have an hour free and are about to read a book you've been enjoying. A close friend messages: “I'm bored—want to hang out?” They just feel like company; nothing urgent has happened. What would you probably reply?

**Response task:** the likely reply, not an explanation of the person's general priorities.

The answer can include joining, declining, postponing, combining the activities, checking what the friend has in mind, or an unanticipated response. Do not turn this into “your needs versus theirs.” Enjoyment of the planned activity is supplied context, not evidence of a solitary personality.

**Applicability:** reading supplies a concrete activity rather than making the respondent invent one. If that activity does not fit, use a familiar alternative and retain its identity rather than pretending all activities are equivalent. This need not become a preliminary hobbies questionnaire. A correction to the scene is not behavioral evidence.

### B. Being around someone who keeps worrying

> You're having a quiet evening at home and feel fairly settled. Your partner has been worrying for several days about going to a gathering where they won't know many people. Tonight they bring it up again, without asking you to do anything. What, if anything, happens to your own mood?

**Response task:** the change, absence of change, or mixed change in the respondent's mood.

Use this relationship role only when applicable, or when the person knowingly chooses to imagine it. A close-friend version is available as a different context, not an interchangeable measurement. Do not infer relationship history from accepting a hypothetical role.

The scene distinguishes recurrent worry from a request to fix a problem. It does not prescribe concern, irritation, anxiety, steadiness, or a helpful action. The fairly settled starting mood is supplied context; it does not establish the person's usual baseline.

**Possible later contrast:** nearby versus elsewhere, without changing the participant's activity to being at work. A strict wording comparison could give the same earlier message in both versions and change only whether the partner is now quietly nearby or elsewhere, with no new information. Even that contrast would describe responses to the scenes, not establish emotional transmission or its cause. The more natural talking-versus-message comparison changes available cues as well as location and must be described accordingly.

### C. A plan changes shortly beforehand

> You were looking forward to meeting a friend this afternoon. Shortly before you would leave, they cancel because they have a mild cold. You haven't spent money or rearranged other commitments. What is your first reaction?

**Response task:** the initial reaction, in the participant's own terms.

Do not require separate emotional, bodily, behavioral, and explanatory answers. If they naturally report several of these, retain them. “I'd check whether they need anything, then enjoy the free afternoon” already gives a sequence; it should not trigger four routine probes.

The scene supplies a canceled plan with limited practical loss. It does not establish that canceled plans are frequent in the person's life, that friends let them down, or that this response applies to a costly cancellation. Ask about a materially different consequence only if it resolves something that actually remains unanswered.

### D. Continuing when an activity becomes repetitive

> A few weeks ago you started learning some phrases in another language for fun. You still like the idea of being able to speak it, but today's practice feels repetitive. You have twenty minutes free. What would you probably do with that time?

**Response task:** the next use of the available time.

Someone could practise, change how they learn, do something else, stop the project, or offer another response. Do not equate any one answer with discipline, laziness, attention problems, or an inability to finish things.

The loss of immediate enjoyment is a premise supplied by this scene. It does not show that the respondent commonly loses interest. Their answer concerns what they predict doing **when** this condition holds. A different familiar learning activity can be used, with the change retained in the record. Obligation-driven work would be a separate context, not a silent substitution.

### E. An interesting opportunity with a commitment

> A nearby group is offering a free cooking class, one evening a week for a month. You like the sound of it, and the time would work for you, but you haven't tried a class with this group before. You need to sign up by tomorrow. What would you probably do next?

**Response task:** the next step, including an immediate answer, seeking information, deferring, or doing nothing.

This is about a bounded decision with uncertainty and an opportunity cost, not whether the person is generally decisive or adventurous. Interest and schedule compatibility are supplied facts. They must not become evidence of novelty-seeking or abundant spare time.

**Applicability and missing information:** cooking supplies an actual subject instead of asking the respondent to invent an interesting opportunity. If it does not fit, use a familiar alternative and retain that version. “I would find out what they teach” can be the person's predicted next step, not a defect to eliminate by supplying every class detail in advance. Distinguish that from being unable to engage with the scene at all. This card remains a candidate to replace if clarification takes more effort than it saves.

### F. A different preference in a small group

> You're deciding where to meet with three friends. Two suggest a place you find too noisy, and you would prefer somewhere quieter. Nothing has been booked. What would you probably say?

**Response task:** what they would say in this disagreement.

The person can negotiate, suggest an alternative, agree, stay silent, question the premise, or do something else. No answer key rewards assertiveness, compliance, tact, or confrontation.

The quiet-place preference belongs to the fictional scene, not to the respondent's profile. Likewise, agreeing with the group does not by itself establish fear of conflict. A boss, parent, or financially dependent relationship would change the context and possible consequences; it must not be substituted as though only the wording changed.

## 4. A worked answer-sensitive branch

This is an authored illustration, not a transcript or a model-quality test.

**Interviewer — Card A**

> You have an hour free and are about to read a book you've been enjoying. A close friend messages: “I'm bored—want to hang out?” They just feel like company; nothing urgent has happened. What would you probably reply?

**Illustrative participant**

> I'd say I could meet them afterwards. But I'd probably spend the hour feeling guilty and wondering whether I'd upset them.

There is no need to ask what they would say, how they would feel, or whether they would prioritize themselves. All three would recycle information. Nor should the interviewer announce, “I've discovered that you set boundaries but feel guilty.” The person just supplied that description.

A potentially useful next turn is:

**Interviewer**

> They reply, “No problem, see you later.” What would the rest of that hour be like for you?

The new supplied condition is the friend's accepting reply. The question does not demand an explanation or require the participant to feel better.

**Illustrative participant — one possible branch**

> Then I'd be fine. I wouldn't keep thinking about it.

**Appropriate next move**

> Let's try a different situation. You were looking forward to meeting a friend this afternoon. Shortly before you would leave, they cancel because they have a mild cold. You haven't spent money or rearranged other commitments. What is your first reaction?

The conversation moves on without forcing a synthesis. A private, source-linked summary could say that in this imagined invitation the respondent expects guilt or concern before a friendly reply, and expects it to subside after that reply. This is a summary of their two answers, not a discovered trait, verified real episode, or proven causal mechanism.

**Alternative reply to the same follow-up**

> I'd still wonder whether they really meant it.

That answer does not license an attachment label or an invented childhood explanation. If one further distinction is worth exploring, the interviewer could ask:

> What about this friend or their reply would leave you unsure?

The person might supply relationship history, an assumption about the fictional friend, uncertainty about the wording, or a broader self-report. These have different meanings. If they already supplied that explanation, skip the question. If the answer remains unclear, it is acceptable to preserve that uncertainty and change topic.

The purpose of this branch is to follow the respondent's actual unresolved point, not to keep changing the scene until they display a desired personality contrast.

## 5. How the next question would be selected

These are proposed conversational decisions, not an implemented state machine or a scoring algorithm.

| What the answer contains | Proposed next move | What must not happen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A request for a decisive missing detail | Supply that detail explicitly and return to the same response task | Treat the request as indecisiveness or evidence sufficiency |
| A conditional answer that already covers both cases | Retain the condition and the branches; move on unless a useful ambiguity remains | Force a single side or re-ask the condition |
| A direct answer with one consequential unanswered distinction | Ask one concrete follow-up, as in the accepting-reply example | Ask a bundle of action, reason, feeling, memory, and frequency |
| An answer that already supplies the would-be follow-up | Skip that probe | Rephrase it and call it deeper exploration |
| A moral rule rather than a personal prediction | Where useful, ask what they expect they would actually say in this scene | Substitute the moral rule for behavior or treat it as deceptive |
| A remembered actual incident | Follow the volunteered incident when helpful, keeping the fictional prompt separate | Invent an incident to fill an evidence requirement |
| An unfamiliar situation or inability to predict | Offer a suitable different scene; a recent example can be invited but is not required | Force imagination or recollection, or code unfamiliarity as personality |
| Criticism, confusion, or a correction | Repair the question or attribution | Treat feedback as a life-pattern fact, consent to stop, or a reason to repeat the same synthesis |
| A clear request to stop | Stop and preserve what was supplied | Continue to complete a card or coverage quota |

A possible opening sequence for discussion is A, then C or D, followed by another appropriate family. It is not fixed administration order. The partner scene need not come early, and no background questionnaire is needed merely to reach it. Reuse relationship applicability already volunteered; otherwise ask one short applicability question only when that scene is worth using.

Prefer breadth over repeatedly interrogating one ordinary response. Use a contrast only when the prior answer gives it a purpose. “We have not yet asked this version” is not sufficient justification. Do not optimize selection for an undisclosed target chart or for answers that sound especially distinctive.

## 6. When a pattern is worth discussing

A clear conditional self-description is already useful; it need not be promoted into a revelation. A pattern discussion may be worthwhile when the participant identifies a recurring relation, or when genuinely separate reports suggest a connection whose added content can be made explicit.

For example, merely combining “I feel guilty after saying no” with “I worry after disagreeing” does not automatically establish a common cause. A question about a possible connection must remain an invitation to judge it, with room to reject it or keep the situations separate. Do not force cross-scene synthesis because the interview has reached a certain number of cards.

Keep source meaning at the claim level, including mixed replies. A participant may answer one scene with a prediction, then volunteer a usual tendency and a particular episode. Those are not competing categories from which the whole answer must choose one.

The supplied situation, anticipated response, usual-behavior self-report, remembered event, retrospective childhood description, normative view, and question feedback remain distinct. Unclear source status can remain unclear. Participant confirmation establishes intended self-description, not independent behavioral validity.

Several variants of one fictional situation remain related hypothetical evidence. They are not several independent real-life occurrences. Neither hypothetical answer counts nor selectively recalled examples establish everyday prevalence without further information about real opportunities.

## 7. Childhood, age, and life stage

For this prototype, start in current life without requesting birth date or exact age. Ask about a role only where it affects the applicability of a useful scene, and do not infer age from an answer as part of selecting questions.

A childhood version can be offered later, not as a mandatory opening or as access to a supposedly truer self:

> Think of a childhood period you remember reasonably well. When another child wanted you to come and play while you were busy with something you had chosen, what do you remember usually doing?

This asks for a retrospective usual-behavior report. A volunteered particular memory stays a reported event; an imagined answer about being five stays an imaginative self-description. None should be relabeled to obtain a stronger evidence class. “I don't remember” is sufficient; no effort to recover a memory is required. Early autobiographical recall has known limitations, but that does not make all childhood reports false. [3]

A broad age band might eventually help, but this pass does not establish that it is necessary. Life-stage or relationship information can itself reveal age-related clues. Later birth-date/time recovery must not count disclosed age, school dates, or other calendar clues as success derived from behavior. Merely separating a demographic form does not eliminate clues in narrative text. Any later use of such information needs its own declared evaluation conditions; no such analysis is authorized here.

## 8. The next useful owner observation

The unresolved question is whether these exact scenes are easier for the owner to answer and whether the follow-up feels attentive rather than repetitive. This authored draft cannot establish that.

A text-only walkthrough, when the owner chooses to try it, can begin with A and follow the actual answer. It need not run all six cards. Watch for the first decisive missing assumption, an unfamiliar setting, a bundled demand, or a repeated question. Record the defect without turning the owner's reaction into personality evidence. An informal owner walkthrough is development feedback, not a validation study. Cognitive interviewing provides an established way to examine comprehension and response processes; this document borrows that concern rather than claiming to have carried out the method. [1]

Keep a well-written recent-event prompt as a genuine simpler alternative:

> Think of the last ordinary time someone asked for your company while you were busy. What did you say?

If the scenario keeps requiring a chain of clarifications while a recent example is readily available, change the entry method for that topic. Do not keep adding hypothetical branches just because scenarios were the proposed direction. Conversely, a real example should not become compulsory when the person can answer the scene but cannot retrieve an incident.

One response task per turn is the working design choice. It adapts the established question-page principle without imposing a new Continue button or a government-form interface. [2] Full usability, accessibility, reliability, population coverage, and research validation remain unassessed.
