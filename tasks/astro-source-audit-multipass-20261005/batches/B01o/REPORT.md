## 1. The general enquiry has two separate organizing axes

II.1 distinguishes the general enquiry about peoples, countries and cities from the genethlialogical enquiry about individuals. Ptolemy assigns priority to general matters because, in his causal account, stronger and more general causes prevail over weaker and more particular ones (PDF141–143/printed117–119).

Within the general enquiry, he then makes two divisions that must not be conflated:

- **Spatial scope:** whole countries versus cities. Robbins records a variant in which the latter concerns both countries and cities.
- **Kind of circumstance:** greater and more periodic conditions, illustrated by wars, famines, pestilences, earthquakes and deluges; and lesser, more occasional variation, illustrated by temperature, storms, heat, winds and crops.

The source prefers country-level and more important conditions first. It does not turn those two axes into a scalar score, specify periods for the supposedly periodic events, or give numerical thresholds separating the classes. These omissions remain explicit in the extraction.

The methodological implication for our later work is limited: general or shared circumstances cannot automatically be counted as individual chart-specific evidence. The chapters do not demonstrate that their proposed celestial explanations successfully predict those circumstances. The existing project distinction between source extraction, development fitting and untouched evaluation is unchanged.

## 2. The same technical words have context-specific meanings

At the end of II.1, Ptolemy says two things are particularly considered: the familiarity of signs and stars with regions, and heavenly configurations at the relevant time. The latter names ecliptical conjunctions of the Sun and Moon and planetary passages at rising and stations. This introductory sentence is not a complete event-selection or timing algorithm (PDF145/printed121).

Robbins's notes make five meanings explicit:

| Term and local context | Meaning supplied in the note |
|---|---|
| Climes, II.1 note 1 | Latitudes or regions determined by latitude |
| Proper regions, II.1 note 2 | Such as planetary houses, referring to I.17, or terms, referring to I.20–I.21 |
| Transits, II.1 note 3 | Passage of a heavenly body through the zodiac |
| Parallels, II.2 note 5 | Latitude: north/south position |
| Angles, II.2 note 5 | East/west position |

Thus “angles” here cannot silently inherit a natal Ascendant/Midheaven definition. Likewise, the house reference in the proper-regions note points to the planets' houses in Book I, not automatically to a natal house-division algorithm. These are local definitions, not claims that the words have only one meaning throughout the book.

The reference reader now requires the source context alongside the word. An unknown context yields an explicit error instead of borrowing the closest familiar definition. No coordinates, people or natal charts are classified.

## 3. The regional portraits remain historical claims, including their unfavorable content

II.2 offers a latitude-based comparison of a southern region, a northern region and an intermediate region. It then discusses the southernmost members of the intermediate group and contrasts eastern and western subgroups. The asserted mechanisms include solar position, heat, moisture and solar/lunar orientation. The portraits mix bodily characteristics, character descriptions, social valuation and supposed aptitude (PDF145–151/printed121–127).

All six portraits are retained with their prerequisites and attribution. Their favorable and unfavorable content has not been rewritten into a uniformly positive reading, and their historical general names have not been equated with present-day states or populations. The text's stereotypes do not become modern ethnic, religious, personality or clinical classifiers in this project.

Two provenance distinctions matter. The main text's southernmost-intermediate passage does not itself name Egyptians and Chaldaeans; that identification and the interpretation concerning the discovery of astrology come from the Anonymous commentator as reported by Robbins. The adopted eastern “frankness” wording also has two reported interpretations—freedom of speech and felicity of expression. They remain alternative readings, not three independent traits (PDF149/printed125).

Robbins's proposed dependence on Posidonius is expressly **probable**. We retained that uncertainty; this batch did not inspect the works of Posidonius, Boll or Ptolemy's *Geography* to adjudicate it (PDF145/printed121).

## 4. The closing qualification does not allow uniform application to every person

The last paragraph recognizes local variation even inside broad hot, cold and temperate regions. It names situation, height, lowness and adjacency, then gives examples linking plains to horsemanship, proximity to the sea to seamanship, and rich soil to civilization. It uses these as analogies for local celestial familiarity. They are the author's examples, not measured effects produced by this audit (PDF151/printed127).

The paragraph expressly says the traits are generally present **but not in every individual**. That is a real scope limitation in the source. It does not supply a prevalence, a calibrated probability, or a rule for selecting the exceptions.

For later implementation, therefore, “generally” cannot silently mean “always,” but neither can the exception clause be used after seeing a mismatch to count that mismatch as a success. That evaluation implication is our research-method inference, not a new statistical claim by Ptolemy.
