## Four methodological clusters and their contradictions

Lilly grades social status outcomes under several overlapping methods: essential dignities (own house/exaltation/mutual reception strongest, triplicity/term/face intermediate, peregrine/detriment/fall weak); angular vs succedent vs cadent placements (with an anomalous grouping of **eighth house with cadent**, despite eighth technically succedent); planets/rulers of first and tenth; source **regal fixed stars**; special time luminaries (Sun diurnal, Moon nocturnal), and several royal configurations involving signs, aspects, fixed stars and exact birth moments. The chapter's many alternative compound conditions have no unique priority or calibrated probability; original zodiac glyphs in several examples remain unresolved and are not computationally executable.

The source explicitly says **not to deliver a positive categorical judgment from one aphorism**, then later claims an extraordinarily narrow noon/Sun-entering-Aries rule could indicate greatness without further testimony. This internal contradiction must be preserved rather than resolved retrospectively. It also states astrologically favorable configurations may correspond either to permanent office or sudden elevation followed by a fall; prestige, salary, inheritance, public role, and peer reputation are separate outcomes.

## Most important social-status counterexample

Lilly directly warns that **a beggar's child and a king's child can have equal-looking natal configurations, but their social ranks and opportunities differ**. The same extraordinarily fortunate astrological direction might count as royal command or public distinction for the king, while the beggar receives additional alms or unusual esteem among peers. This is an explicit change of outcome reference class and is scientifically a major source of unconstrained retrospective fitting. A future test must freeze before exposure whether the predicted target is absolute government rank, wealth level, within-class advancement, peer esteem, or subjective satisfaction, and must compare with strong baseline predictors for family class and opportunities. A rule that permits all outcomes at once is not a strong numerical prediction.
